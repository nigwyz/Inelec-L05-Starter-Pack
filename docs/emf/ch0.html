How the Pieces Fit

EMF looks like a wall of formulas. It is actually one pipeline: choose coordinates → build the differential element → integrate or apply a theorem → read the field. This page is the map, the ten rules, and the diagnostic that tells you where to start.

1The big picture in one paragraph

Charges create fields. The field at a point is a vector, so every problem starts with geometry: where is the source, where is the observation point, and what is the vector between them. Coulomb and Biot–Savart give the field of a tiny source. To add up a whole line, surface, or volume of sources, you integrate. If the source distribution has symmetry, Gauss's law replaces the integral with one multiplication. If the field is the gradient of a potential, the vector problem collapses to a scalar one. Maxwell's four equations tie the electric and magnetic fields together and predict waves.

That is the whole course. Chapters 1–2 build the language, Chapter 3 applies it to stationary charges, Chapter 4 to steady currents, and Chapters 5–6 to time-varying fields and waves.

The one sentence to remember

A field problem is a source element, a vector from the source to the observation point, and the right metric factor inside the integral.

What the control actually asks. The 2024–25 paper has four parts: (1) three short questions on conversion, Gauss's law, magnetic force, and the field of a wire; (2) a six-point flux + divergence-theorem exercise in spherical coordinates; (3) a two-point volume-charge integral; (4) a four-point electric field of two point charges. Total 15 points, 1 h 30. The 2021–22 paper has the same spine: flux + divergence theorem (10), field of three charges (2), and Stokes (3).

4The ten rules

These are the recurring moves in the professor's solutions. They are not a substitute for the derivation; they are the checklist you run while solving.

Rule 1

Draw the point before the vector

Mark the origin, the source point Q(x′,y′,z′), the observation point P(x,y,z), and the vector R = r − r′. The direction of every field comes from this picture.

If you cannot point to R, you cannot get the sign right.

Rule 2

Convert the point first, then the vector

A vector's components depend on where it sits. Compute ρ, φ, r, θ at the point, then multiply by the transformation matrix evaluated at that point. Never use the origin's unit vectors at P.

F = 10x̂ at P(ρ=2, φ=30°)
→ F = 10(cos30° ρ̂ − sin30° φ̂)
Rule 3

Dot is projection, cross is area

A·B = AB cosθ gives the component of B along A. |A×B| = AB sinθ gives the area of the parallelogram and a vector normal to both. Use the determinant for components; use the geometry for a sanity check.

Rule 4

Every dl carries its metric factor

Cartesian: (dx, dy, dz). Cylindrical: (dρ, ρdφ, dz). Spherical: (dr, rdθ, r sinθ dφ). The factor is not optional: it is the physical length of the coordinate step.

Wrong factor → wrong integral → wrong units.

Rule 5

Every dS carries the Jacobian

Cylindrical: ρ dρ dφ on the z-face, ρ dφ dz on the ρ-face. Spherical: r² sinθ dθ dφ on the r-face. On a closed surface, the normal points outward.

dS_r = r² sinθ dθ dφ r̂
Rule 6

Divergence is flux per volume

∇·D = ρ_v is Gauss's law at a point. Positive divergence means a source; negative means a sink; zero means incompressible. The integral form sums the flux over a closed surface.

Rule 7

Curl is circulation per area

∇×E = 0 for static fields: the line integral between two points is path-independent. A nonzero curl means the field rotates; the paddle-wheel test gives the direction.

Rule 8

Two theorems, two pairings

Divergence: closed surface ↔ enclosed volume. Stokes: closed loop ↔ any open surface bounded by it. Orientation follows the right-hand rule; reversing the normal reverses the sign.

∮D·dS = ∫∇·D dv
∮E·dl = ∫(∇×E)·dS
Rule 9

Gauss only helps with symmetry

Use a Gaussian surface on which D is constant and normal: a sphere for a point/sphere, a cylinder for a line, a pillbox for a plane. Otherwise, integrate Coulomb directly.

Rule 10

Potential is the scalar shortcut

E = −∇V and V = −∫E·dl. Potentials add as scalars, so find V first when many charges are present, then take one gradient. Set V=0 at infinity for finite distributions.

The two most expensive mistakes in the archive. (1) Forgetting the r² sinθ in a spherical volume integral. (2) Using the wrong sign for the outward normal on the inner surface of a shell. Both turn a correct method into a wrong number, and both are visible in the 2024–25 solution.

5The six-step solver

Every control question can be attacked in the same order. Write the steps on the back of your mind, not on the paper.

1 · Name the target

Is the answer a scalar (Q, flux, V) or a vector (E, D, B, F)? A vector answer needs a direction check at the end; a scalar answer needs a sign check.

2 · Choose coordinates

Spherical for a point or sphere, cylindrical for a wire or cylinder, Cartesian for a box or a general point. The symmetry of the source decides, not the symmetry of the question text.

3 · Write the source element

Point: dq. Line: ρ_l dl. Surface: ρ_s dS. Volume: ρ_v dv. Current: I dl. Write the metric factors immediately.

4 · Build R and the field

R = r − r′, R = |R|. Then dE = dq R/(4πε0 R³) or dB = μ0 I dl×R/(4πR³). Keep the vector signs until the last step.

5 · Integrate or apply the theorem

Set the limits from the geometry. If the surface is closed, split it into faces and compute each flux. If the field is symmetric, use Gauss or Ampère and skip the integral.

6 · Check units, sign, direction

E in V/m, D in C/m², B in T, flux in C or Wb, potential in V. A positive charge has E pointing away; a negative charge has E pointing toward it. If the direction is wrong, the magnitude does not save you.

Use this on every worked example. When you read a solution, cover it and run the six steps aloud. If you cannot name the step where the solution made a choice, you have not learned the method yet.

6Diagnostic — five questions before you start

Answer without notes. If you miss more than two, start at Chapter 1. If you miss two, skim Chapter 1 and start Chapter 2. If you miss none, go straight to the timed control.

1 · Chapter 1
At the point P(x=1, y=1, z=0), what is the unit vector φ̂ in Cartesian components?
Show answer
φ̂ = −sinφ x̂ + cosφ ŷ. At (1,1), φ=45°, so φ̂ = (−1/√2, 1/√2, 0). Answer b.
2 · Chapter 2
For D = 3r² r̂, what is ∇·D?
Show answer
∇·D = (1/r²) ∂(r²·3r²)/∂r = (1/r²) ∂(3r⁴)/∂r = (1/r²)(12r³) = 12r. Answer c.
3 · Chapter 2
A closed surface encloses a volume in which ∇·D = 12(r + 1/(4πr sinθ)). The surface flux equals…
Show answer
Divergence theorem: ∮D·dS = ∫∇·D dv. Answer a.
4 · Chapter 3
A volume charge density ρ_v = k r cosθ fills a sphere of radius a. What is the total charge?
Show answer
The cosθ factor makes the positive and negative hemispheres cancel: ∫₀^π cosθ sinθ dθ = 0. Answer d.
5 · Chapter 4
A charge q moves with velocity v parallel to a uniform magnetic field B. The magnetic force is…
Show answer
F = qv×B. If v ∥ B, the cross product is zero. Answer b.
