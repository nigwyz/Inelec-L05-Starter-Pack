Chapter 04 · Magnetostatics

Magnetostatics

A steady current creates a magnetic field. That field pushes moving charges and other currents. The exam reduces this to three moves: build B from a current element, use symmetry to avoid the integral, and evaluate a cross product without losing the direction.

On this page
  1. Biot–Savart law
  2. Ampère's circuital law
  3. Magnetic force
  4. Gauss's law for B
  5. Check yourself
What this chapter unlocks. The 2024–25 control uses magnetostatics in its short-questions block: one question asks for the field of an infinite filament, another evaluates qv×B. Final-style problems add coax, solenoid and flux variants. The patterns are narrow and repeatable, so treat every formula below as an exam recipe, not a museum piece.

4.1Biot–Savart: the field of a current element

Electrostatics starts from a charge element and Coulomb's inverse-square law. Magnetostatics starts from a current element and the Biot–Savart law. The geometry is the same: a source at a point, a field point at a distance R, and an inverse-square falloff. The new ingredient is a cross product, because the magnetic field has a direction that is perpendicular to both the current and the line joining source to field point.

dB⃗ = μ0 / 4π · (I dℓ⃗ × âR) / R²
= (μ0 / 4π) · (I dℓ⃗ × R⃗) / R³

Read it in three pieces:

  • Source. I dℓ⃗ points in the direction of the current. The element is small enough that R and âR are effectively constant across it.
  • Geometry. âR points from the source element to the field point. R is the length of that vector. Do not confuse the unit vector with the position vector: the first has no units, the second has metres.
  • Direction. The cross product makes dB⃗ perpendicular to the plane containing dℓ⃗ and R⃗. Its magnitude carries a factor sin θ, where θ is the angle between them. If the element points straight at the field point, the field is zero.

Notation: the line element is written dℓ⃗ here so it cannot be confused with the current I. The lecture's dl⃗ is exactly the same quantity.

Right-hand rule, without hand gymnastics. Point the fingers of your right hand along I dℓ⃗, then curl them toward R⃗. Your thumb points along dB⃗. Equivalently, dℓ⃗ × âR: index finger along dℓ⃗, middle finger along âR, thumb along dB⃗. For a wire along +z and a field point at +x, the field is in the +y direction: ẑ × x̂ = ŷ.
Four mistakes that cost marks.
  • Reversing the cross product. dℓ⃗ × âR and âR × dℓ⃗ differ by a minus sign. Use dℓ⃗ × âR; the arrow on R points toward the field point.
  • Using the scalar R in the numerator. The vector form is dℓ⃗ × R⃗ / R³; the unit-vector form is dℓ⃗ × âR / R². They are equivalent because R⃗ = R âR. Do not mix the two: dℓ⃗ × R / R² has no direction.
  • Forgetting the medium. In free space μ0 = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m. In a linear magnetic material replace it by μ = μ0μr. The H in H/m is the henry, not the magnetic field intensity; the same unit is T·m/A.
  • Mixing B and H. B is magnetic flux density in tesla; H is magnetic field intensity in A/m. In free space B = μ0H.
One current element, one field pointdrag θ and R; watch |dB| collapse when dℓ points at P

The element alone is not a field. A real wire is a chain of elements, so you integrate over the source:

B⃗ = (μ0 / 4π) ∫C (I dl⃗ × R⃗) / R³

For a volume current density Jv (A/m²), replace I dℓ⃗ by Jv dv. For a surface current density Js (A/m), replace it by Js ds. The integral is a vector integral: integrate each component separately, then combine.

The finite wire, worked from the integral

Put a filament on the z-axis from z′ = a to z′ = b. Evaluate the field at a point in the xy-plane, a perpendicular distance ρ from the wire. The source is dℓ⃗ = dz′ ẑ; the vector from source to field point is R⃗ = ρ ρ̂ − z′ ẑ. Then

ẑ × (ρ ρ̂ − z′ ẑ) = ρ φ̂
B⃗ = (μ0 I ρ / 4π) ∫ab dz′ / (ρ² + z′²)3/2 · φ̂
= (μ0 I / 4πρ) [ b / √(ρ² + b²) − a / √(ρ² + a²) ] φ̂

For a segment symmetric about the plane z′ = 0, from −L to +L, the bracket becomes 2L / √(ρ² + L²). Letting L → ∞ sends both fractions to 1, so the bracket tends to 2 and the infinite-wire result appears:

B⃗ = μ0 I / (2πρ) φ̂    (infinite straight wire)

The direction is the azimuthal unit vector φ̂, not ρ̂. The field circles the wire. Reverse the current and every circle reverses.

Infinite wire: B falls as 1/ρchange the distance, then flip the current

Worked example — infinite wire

A long straight wire carries I = 25 A along +z. Find B and H at ρ = 4 cm on the +x-axis.

B = μ0I / (2πρ) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(25) / (2π×0.04)
= (2×10⁻⁷ × 25) / 0.04 = 5×10⁻⁶ / 0.04 = 1.25×10⁻⁴ T = 125 µT
H = I / (2πρ) = 25 / (2π×0.04) = 99.5 A/m

At (4 cm, 0, 0), φ̂ = ŷ, so B⃗ = 125 µT ŷ. Check: B = μ0H = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 99.5 = 1.25×10⁻⁴ T.

Worked example — finite segment

A filament from z = −4 cm to z = +4 cm carries I = 10 A. Find B at ρ = 3 cm in the mid-plane.

μ0I / (4πρ) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(10) / (4π×0.03) = 1×10⁻⁶ / 0.03 = 3.333×10⁻⁵ T
b / √(ρ² + b²) = 0.04 / √(0.03² + 0.04²) = 0.04 / 0.05 = 0.8
a / √(ρ² + a²) = −0.04 / 0.05 = −0.8
B = 3.333×10⁻⁵ × (0.8 − (−0.8)) = 5.33×10⁻⁵ T = 53.3 µT

The infinite wire at the same distance would give μ0I/(2πρ) = 66.7 µT. The finite segment gives 80% of that. The factor is 2L/√(ρ²+L²) = 2(4)/5 = 1.6, and 1.6/2 = 0.8. As the segment gets longer, the factor approaches 2 and the infinite-wire value is recovered.

Loops, solenoids, toroids

For a circular loop of radius a carrying I, the field on the axis at height z is

Bz = μ0 I a² / [2(a² + z²)3/2]   →   Bcenter = μ0I / (2a)

A solenoid is a stack of loops. Far from the ends, the contributions add to a nearly uniform field inside and a weak field outside. If the solenoid has N turns over length L, the turn density is n = N/L:

B = μ0 n I = μ0 (N/L) I    (inside an ideal long solenoid)
B = μ0 N I / (2πρ)    (inside a toroid, at radius ρ)

The direction inside a solenoid follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the winding current; the thumb points along B inside.

Exam pattern · Control 2024–25, Question 4. An infinite filament along the z-axis carries I. At any point in the xy-plane the answer is B⃗ = μ0I/(2πρ) φ̂. The mark is for the direction as much as the magnitude. Practise the same question in the control set.

4.2Ampère's circuital law: the symmetry shortcut

Biot–Savart always works, but the integral can be ugly. Ampère's law trades the integral over the source for an integral around a closed path. When the path follows the field, the line integral collapses to a product and the field falls out algebraically.

∮C H⃗ · dℓ⃗ = Ienc
B⃗ = μH⃗,    μ0 = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m

The sign convention is the right-hand rule: choose a direction around C, curl the fingers of your right hand that way, and your thumb points along the positive current. A current flowing with the thumb counts as positive; one flowing the other way counts as negative. The enclosed current is the net current through any surface bounded by C.

When Ampère is useful. You need enough symmetry that H is either constant on the path and parallel to dℓ⃗, or perpendicular to it. Then the integral becomes H × (path length) or zero. The four standard cases are the infinite wire, the coaxial cable, the long solenoid, and the toroid. If the source is a short wire or a loop, use Biot–Savart instead.
GeometryAmperian pathHB in free space
Infinite straight wire, current ICircle of radius ρ centred on the wireI/(2πρ)μ0I/(2πρ)
Coax, between the conductorsCircle of radius ρ around the inner conductorI/(2πρ)μ0I/(2πρ)
Long solenoid, n turns per metreRectangular path, one side inside, one outsidenIμ0nI
Toroid, N turns, radius ρ inside the coreCircle of radius ρ inside the coreNI/(2πρ)μ0NI/(2πρ)
Ampère's loop around a wirechange I and ρ; verify ∮H·dℓ = I every time

For the circular path, H is tangent everywhere and has the same magnitude at every point. Therefore H⃗ · dℓ⃗ = H dℓ, and

∮ H⃗ · dℓ⃗ = H ∮ dℓ = H(2πρ) = [I/(2πρ)](2πρ) = I

The radius cancels. That is why the 1/ρ in H is not an independent experimental fact: it is forced by the geometry of the circular path.

Worked example — coaxial cable

An inner conductor of radius a = 1 mm carries I = 10 A out of the page. A thin outer conductor at b = 4 mm carries the return current into the page. Find H and B at ρ = 0.5 mm, 2.5 mm and 5 mm.

RegionIencHB at the stated point
Inside the inner conductor, ρ < aI(ρ²/a²)Iρ/(2πa²)H = 10(0.0005)/(2π×10⁻⁶) = 795.8 A/m
B = μ0H = 1.00 mT
Between the conductors, a < ρ < bII/(2πρ)H = 10/(2π×0.0025) = 636.6 A/m
B = 0.800 mT
Outside the outer conductor, ρ > bI − I = 00H = 0, B = 0

Check the middle region directly: H(2πρ) = 636.6 × 2π × 0.0025 = 10.0 A, which is exactly the enclosed current. At ρ = 5 mm the path encloses +10 A and −10 A, so the net is zero and the field outside is zero. If the outer conductor were thick, you would subtract only the fraction of the return current enclosed by the path; at a radius inside the metal, the field is generally not zero.

Worked example — solenoid check

A solenoid has N = 500 turns over L = 20 cm and carries I = 3 A. Find n, H and B inside, far from the ends.

n = N/L = 500 / 0.20 = 2500 turns/m
H = nI = 2500 × 3 = 7500 A/m
B = μ0H = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 7500 = 9.42×10⁻³ T = 9.42 mT

For a toroid with the same 500 turns, carrying 3 A at ρ = 5 cm: B = μ0NI/(2πρ) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(500)(3)/(2π×0.05) = 6.0 mT. The toroid confines the field to its core; the solenoid has a weak return field outside.

Common mistakes with Ampère.
  • Using the total current instead of the enclosed current. The path must be closed, and only current through a surface bounded by that path counts. For a coax outside both conductors, Ienc = I − I = 0.
  • Assuming symmetry that is not there. Ampère gives one algebraic equation only when H is constant on the path and tangent to it. A finite wire does not qualify; a circular path around its midpoint does not have constant H.
  • Writing ∮B·dℓ = I. The law is ∮H·dℓ = Ienc. If you use B, the right side becomes μ0Ienc in free space.
  • Forgetting the return current. A coax is not an infinite wire plus a separate shell; the outer conductor carries the return current, and that changes Ienc outside it.
Exam pattern · final-style coax/solenoid question. The marker looks for three things: the correct Amperian path, the correct Ienc, and the region-by-region answer. Write the regions explicitly, even when the answer is zero. The same skill is drilled in the mixed practice set.

4.3Magnetic force on a charge and on a current

A magnetic field does not push a stationary charge. It pushes a charge only when the charge moves, and the push is sideways to both the velocity and the field. The defining equation is

F⃗m = q v⃗ × B⃗    (N)
|Fm| = |q| v B sin θ

For a positive charge, F⃗ is along v⃗ × B⃗. For a negative charge, reverse it. The force is perpendicular to v⃗, so it does no work and cannot change the speed; it only bends the path. It is also perpendicular to B⃗, so a charge moving parallel to B feels nothing. The maximum force occurs at θ = 90°.

If an electric field is also present, add the electric force:

F⃗ = q(E⃗ + v⃗ × B⃗)    (Lorentz force)
F = qv × B in three dimensionschange Δ, then flip the sign of q

For a current-carrying wire, each charge carrier contributes. The net result for a straight segment of length L is

F⃗ = I L⃗ × B⃗    or    dF⃗ = I dℓ⃗ × B⃗

L⃗ points in the direction of the current, with magnitude equal to the length. For a curved wire in a uniform field, the total force depends only on the vector from the start to the end of the wire; the shape cancels. That is why a semicircular arc has the same force as the straight chord joining its endpoints.

A quick numerical check: a straight wire of length 0.50 m carries 8 A perpendicular to a uniform 0.20 T field. Then F = ILB = (8)(0.50)(0.20) = 0.80 N, with the direction from L⃗ × B⃗. If the wire were parallel to B, the force would be zero.

Worked example — the 2024–25 control, Question 3

A point charge q = 30 nC moves with speed 2×10⁶ m/s in the direction v̂ = 0.60x̂ + 0.75ŷ + 0.30ẑ. The magnetic flux density is B⃗ = 2x̂ − 4ŷ + 3ẑ mT. Find v⃗ × B⃗, F⃗ and |F|.

Step 1 — write the vectors with their powers of ten.

v⃗ = 2×10⁶ (0.60x̂ + 0.75ŷ + 0.30ẑ) = (1.20x̂ + 1.50ŷ + 0.60ẑ)×10⁶ m/s
B⃗ = (2x̂ − 4ŷ + 3ẑ)×10⁻³ T

Step 2 — expand the determinant. The powers combine as 10⁶ × 10⁻³ = 10³.

v⃗ × B⃗ = x̂[(1.50)(3) − (0.60)(−4)]×10³
+ ŷ[(0.60)(2) − (1.20)(3)]×10³
+ ẑ[(1.20)(−4) − (1.50)(2)]×10³
= x̂(4.50 + 2.40)×10³ + ŷ(1.20 − 3.60)×10³ + ẑ(−4.80 − 3.00)×10³
= (6.90x̂ − 2.40ŷ − 7.80ẑ)×10³ T·m/s

Step 3 — multiply by q. q = 30×10⁻⁹ C, so q × 10³ = 30×10⁻⁶.

F⃗ = 30×10⁻⁶ (6.90x̂ − 2.40ŷ − 7.80ẑ)
= (2.07x̂ − 0.720ŷ − 2.34ẑ)×10⁻⁴ N
= (207x̂ − 72ŷ − 234ẑ) µN

Step 4 — magnitude.

|F| = √(207² + 72² + 234²) µN = √(42849 + 5184 + 54756) µN
= √102789 µN = 320.6 µN

Step 5 — check the directions. The force must be perpendicular to both v and B:

F⃗ · v⃗ ∝ (207)(0.60) + (−72)(0.75) + (−234)(0.30) = 124.2 − 54.0 − 70.2 = 0 ✓
F⃗ · B⃗ = (207)(2) + (−72)(−4) + (−234)(3) = 414 + 288 − 702 = 0 ✓

The unit direction is F⃗/|F| = 0.646x̂ − 0.225ŷ − 0.730ẑ. Notice that the force is not along B and not along v. That is the most common conceptual error in this question.

Two traps in the printed question and key.

The vector (0.60, 0.75, 0.30) has length √1.0125 = 1.0062, so it is not exactly a unit vector even though the paper calls it v̂. The official key multiplies 2×10⁶ by the printed components directly; that convention gives the official 320.6 µN. If you first normalize the direction to unit length, you get 318.6 µN. In an exam, state which convention you used; here we follow the key so the number matches.

The key's bracket (3.45, −1.2, −3.9) still carries the millitesla factor. Multiplying it by qv = 60×10⁻³ alone gives (0.207, −0.072, −0.234) N, which is 320.6 mN. Include the 10⁻³ from B and the components become (207, −72, −234) µN, with magnitude 320.6 µN.

Cyclotron motion

When v is perpendicular to a uniform B, the magnetic force is always perpendicular to v and has constant magnitude. It supplies the centripetal force:

|q|vB = mv²/r   →   r = mv/(|q|B)
T = 2πm/(|q|B),    ω = |q|B/m

For a proton (m = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C) at v = 2.0×10⁶ m/s in B = 0.50 T: r = (1.67×10⁻²⁷)(2.0×10⁶)/[(1.60×10⁻¹⁹)(0.50)] = 4.2 cm. The period is independent of speed in the non-relativistic limit, which is the principle of the cyclotron.

Force between parallel wires

Wire 1 creates a field at wire 2. Wire 2 then feels a force. For two long parallel wires separated by d,

B1 = μ0I1/(2πd)
F/L = I2B1 = μ0 I1I2/(2πd)

Same-direction currents attract; opposite-direction currents repel. This is the opposite of the rule for point charges, and it is a favourite true/false trap.

Two parallel wires: attract or repel?flip the second current, then change d

Worked example — parallel wires

Two long parallel wires carry I1 = I2 = 10 A in the same direction, separated by d = 4 cm. Find the field of wire 1 at wire 2 and the force per unit length.

B1 = μ0I1/(2πd) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(10)/(2π×0.04) = 5×10⁻⁵ T = 50 µT
F/L = I2B1 = 10 × 5×10⁻⁵ = 5×10⁻⁴ N/m = 500 µN/m

Because the currents are in the same direction, the force is attractive. Doubling d to 8 cm halves the force to 250 µN/m.

Common mistakes with magnetic force.
  • Putting F along B. The magnetic force is perpendicular to B and to v. Only the electric force is parallel to its field.
  • Forgetting the sign of q. A negative charge feels the force opposite to v × B. Electrons curve the other way.
  • Dropping the milli. 1 mT = 10⁻³ T. In the control question, the factor 10⁻³ is exactly what turns the raw cross product into 320.6 µN rather than 320.6 mN.
  • Reversing the cross product. v × B is not B × v. The latter flips the force direction.
  • Using the charge rule for currents. Same-direction currents attract; opposite-direction currents repel. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.
Exam pattern · Control 2024–25, Question 3. This is a pure qv×B calculation with a 3D determinant. The paper awards marks for the cross product, the multiplication by q, the magnitude, and the unit conversion from mT. Reproduce it from a blank page in the control set.

4.4Magnetic flux and Gauss's law for B

Magnetic flux is the amount of B passing through a surface. For an open surface S with a chosen normal,

Φ = ∫S B⃗ · dS⃗    (Wb)
Φ = BA cos θ    (uniform B, flat area A)

The unit is the weber: 1 Wb = 1 T·m². The angle θ is between B and the surface normal dS⃗, not between B and the surface itself. If B is parallel to the surface, no flux passes through it; if B is parallel to the normal, the flux is maximum.

Now close the surface. Every field line that enters must leave, because there is no magnetic charge for it to terminate on. The net flux through any closed surface is therefore zero:

∮S B⃗ · dS⃗ = 0   →   ∇ · B⃗ = 0

This is Gauss's law for magnetic fields. Its physical content is the absence of magnetic monopoles. B lines have no starts or ends: they close on themselves. The differential form ∇·B = 0 says the same thing locally — the magnetic field is solenoidal.

Worked example — flux through a loop

A uniform field B = 0.25 T passes through a circular loop of radius 5 cm. The normal to the loop makes 60° with B. Find the flux.

A = πr² = π(0.05)² = 7.854×10⁻³ m²
Φ = BA cos 60° = (0.25)(7.854×10⁻³)(0.5)
= 9.82×10⁻⁴ Wb = 0.982 mWb

If the same loop is closed by a flat cap to form a closed surface, the flux through the cap is −0.982 mWb and the net flux through the closed surface is zero. That minus sign is not a fudge; it comes from choosing the outward normal on every patch of a closed surface.

Worked example — open hemisphere

A hemispherical bowl of radius a = 5 cm sits in a uniform field B = 0.25 T, with its axis along B. The curved surface and the flat circular rim together form a closed surface, and the net flux through that closed surface is zero. Therefore the flux through the curved open surface equals the flux through the flat rim:

Φcurved = B(πa²) = (0.25)π(0.05)² = 1.96×10⁻³ Wb

Close the bowl with its flat base and the two contributions cancel: +1.96 mWb through the curved part and −1.96 mWb through the base. Net flux through the closed surface: zero, as Gauss's law for B requires.

Common mistakes with magnetic flux.
  • Thinking zero flux means zero field. A closed surface can have zero net flux while B is strong everywhere on it. The field lines enter and leave; the net count is zero.
  • Using the angle with the surface. Φ = BA cos θ uses the angle between B and the normal. If B is 30° above the plane, the angle with the normal is 60°.
  • Forgetting the sign on a closed surface. dS⃗ points outward everywhere. On a closed surface, outward flux is positive and inward flux is negative.
  • Trying to find B from ∮B·dS = 0. The law gives no information about the magnitude of B; it only constrains its divergence. Use Ampère for B.
Exam pattern · short question and final. The short-questions block likes ∇·B = 0 and the statement that magnetic field lines are closed. A final flux question usually gives a uniform field and a tilted flat surface; compute BA cos θ and watch the normal. See the formula sheet for the side-by-side electric and magnetic laws.

4.5Check yourself

Three questions, one per exam skill. Answer first, then open the solution.

Biot–Savart · 1 min

A long straight wire carries 10 A. What is the magnitude of B at a distance of 2 cm from the wire in free space?

Show answer
B = μ0I/(2πρ) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(10)/(2π×0.02) = 2×10⁻⁶/0.02 = 1.0×10⁻⁴ T = 100 µT. The direction is φ̂, circling the wire according to the right-hand rule.
Ampère · 1 min

A long solenoid has 2000 turns/m and carries 3 A. What is B inside, far from the ends?

Show answer
B = μ0nI = (4π×10⁻⁷)(2000)(3) = 7.54×10⁻³ T = 7.54 mT. First compute H = nI = 6000 A/m, then B = μ0H. Do not multiply by the length unless you are using N instead of n.
Magnetic force · 2 min

A charge q = 2 µC moves along +x̂ at 3×10⁵ m/s in a uniform field B⃗ = 0.4 ẑ T. What is the magnetic force?

Show answer
F⃗ = qv⃗ × B⃗ = (2×10⁻⁶)(3×10⁵)(0.4)(x̂ × ẑ) = 0.24(−ŷ) N. The magnitude is qvB = 0.24 N; the direction is x̂ × ẑ = −ŷ. The force is perpendicular to both v and B, so options (b) and (c) are impossible.
