Chapter 3 · Electrostatics

Electrostatics: from one charge to a whole distribution

Almost every electrostatics question is one move repeated: take the source point, subtract it from the observation point, divide by the cube of that distance, and add. This chapter drills the move until the signs are automatic, then adds Gauss's law and potential as the two shortcuts that replace the integral when symmetry lets you.

Notation. Vectors are bold (E, F, r); unit vectors carry a hat (x̂, ŷ, ẑ, ρ̂, âR). ε0 is free-space permittivity; ε = εrε0 is the medium.

3.1Coulomb's law: the force between two charges

Electrostatics starts with one measured fact: two small charges push or pull each other along the line joining them. The force grows with both charges and falls with the square of the separation. Written for the force that charge 1 exerts on charge 2,

F12 = (1/4πε0) · Q1Q2 / R122 · â12   or   F12 = (1/4πε0) · Q1Q2 (r2 − r1) / |r2 − r1|3

The vector form is the one to memorise. Here r1 locates the source charge, r2 locates the charge that feels the force, and

R12 = r2 − r1   points from the source to the observation point.

If Q1Q2 > 0 the force is along R12 and the charges repel. If the product is negative the force reverses and they attract. Newton's third law then gives F12 = −F21, so the pair never accelerates itself.

For many charges, add the pairwise forces as vectors: F = Σi Fi. This is superposition, and it is the reason the electric field is worth defining in the next section.

Permittivity of free space. The constant in front is not a nuisance, it is the medium: ε0 = 10−9/(36π) F/m = 8.842×10−12 F/m ≈ 8.854×10−12 F/m, and 1/(4πε0) ≈ 9×109 m/F (8.988×109 from the full SI definition). In a material replace ε0 by ε = εrε0; in free space εr = 1.

The exam uses the rounded 9×109 because the numbers were built for it: 36π cancels 1/(4π×10−9), which is why 100 nC/m at 2 m later gives exactly 450 V/m. Use 9×109 by hand and say so.

Worked example — the force on the negative charge

Two point charges sit in free space: Q1 = +20 nC at (1, 0, 0) and Q2 = −20 nC at (0, 1, 0), with distances in metres. Find the force on Q2.

1. Source vector. R12 = (0−1, 1−0, 0−0) = (−1, 1, 0) m, so R12 = √2 = 1.414 m and R123 = 2√2 = 2.828 m3.

2. Charge product. Q1Q2 = (20×10−9)(−20×10−9) = −4×10−16 C2. Multiply by 9×109: the numerator is −3.6×10−6 N·m2.

3. Divide by the cube.

F12 = (−3.6×10−6) · (−1, 1, 0) / 2.828 = (1.273, −1.273, 0)×10−6 N = 1.27(x̂ − ŷ) µN

4. Read the answer. The magnitude is √2 × 1.273 = 1.80 µN. The direction is (1, −1, 0)/√2, which points from (0, 1, 0) straight at (1, 0, 0): attraction, as expected for opposite signs. The force on Q1 is the same vector negated, so the two forces are equal and opposite.

Exam link. Control 2024–25, Exercise 3 uses exactly this subtraction with two 60 nC charges. The arithmetic is in the field section; the only new step there is that both charges push on the observation point, so you add two vectors.
Four sign traps.
  • R direction. R = robservation − rsource. Reversing it flips every direction. Sketch the arrow before you calculate.
  • Cube, not square. The unit vector already contains one factor of R; dividing by R3 is what leaves the 1/R2 law.
  • Charge sign is separate. Do not "fix" the direction twice: put the sign inside Q1Q2 and let the vector R stay source-to-point.
  • Prefixes. nC = 10−9 C, µC = 10−6 C. Mixing them changes the answer by 1000.

3.2Electric field intensity

A single charge alone feels nothing. Bring a small test charge qt near it and the test charge feels a force that depends on where you put it. Divide the force by the test charge and the dependence on the test charge disappears: what remains is a property of the source, the electric field.

E = limqt→0 F/qt   (V/m)     and conversely    F = qE

For a point charge q at r′, the field at r is the same Coulomb vector with the test charge removed:

E(r) = q (r − r′) / [4πε0 |r − r′|3]   =   q/(4πε0R2) · âR

With several charges, superposition applies because forces add linearly. No charge acts on itself, so the sum runs over every source except the charge at the observation point:

E = Σi=1n qi (r − ri) / [4πε0 |r − ri|3]
Field lines are a bookkeeping device. They start on positive charge, end on negative charge or run to infinity, never cross, and their density indicates |E|. The tangent at any point is the field direction. A positive test charge released from rest moves along the tangent, not along a straight line to the nearest negative charge.
Three charges, one movable probeDrag the probe sliders; the pale lines are traced field lines, the dark arrow is the vector sum

Worked example — three charges at the origin

Place +10 nC at (1, 0, 0), +10 nC at (0, 1, 0) and −10 nC at (0, 0, 1). Find E at the origin. This is deliberately simple geometry so the vector bookkeeping is visible.

SourceqiriRi = −ri|Ri|3Ei = 9×109 qiRi/Ri3
1+10 nC(1, 0, 0)(−1, 0, 0)1−90 x̂ V/m
2+10 nC(0, 1, 0)(0, −1, 0)1−90 ŷ V/m
3−10 nC(0, 0, 1)(0, 0, −1)1+90 ẑ V/m

Add the three columns:

E = −90x̂ − 90ŷ + 90ẑ V/m   →   |E| = 90√3 = 155.9 V/m   and   âE = (−x̂ − ŷ + ẑ)/√3

Notice the third row: the charge is negative, so q3R3 = (−10 nC)(−ẑ) = +10 nC ẑ. A negative source at +ẑ pulls the field toward +ẑ at the origin. Two sign changes, one physical answer. A 1 nC test charge placed at the origin would feel F = qE = (−90, −90, 90) nN.

Exam pattern — Control 2024–25, Exercise 3

Two 60 nC charges sit at P1(4, −2, 7) and P2(4, −7, −2). Find the field at P3(1, 2, 3), then find the points on the y-axis where Ez = 0.

Part (a). Subtract source from observation:

R1 = (1−4, 2+2, 3−7) = (−3, 4, −4),   R1 = √41 = 6.403,   R13 = 41√41 = 262.5
R2 = (1−4, 2+7, 3+2) = (−3, 9, 5),   R2 = √115 = 10.724,   R23 = 115√115 = 1233.2

With 1/(4πε0) = 9×109, the common factor is 9×109 × 60×10−9 = 540 V·m:

E = 540 [ (−3, 4, −4)/262.5 + (−3, 9, 5)/1233.2 ]
= 540 [ (−0.01143, 0.01524, −0.01524) + (−0.00243, 0.00730, 0.00405) ]
= 540 (−0.01386, 0.02254, −0.01119) = (−7.48x̂ + 12.17ŷ − 6.04ẑ) V/m

The magnitude is |E| = √(7.482 + 12.172 + 6.042) = 15.5 V/m. The handwritten key prints 12.16 and −6.03 because it rounds intermediate values; the exact components are 12.17 and −6.04.

Part (b). A general point on the y-axis is (0, y, 0). Then

R1 = (−4, y+2, −7),   R12 = 65 + (y+2)2;    R2 = (−4, y+7, 2),   R22 = 20 + (y+7)2

Set the z-component of the sum to zero. Since both charges are equal, their common factor cancels:

−7/R13 + 2/R23 = 0  ⟹  2R13 = 7R23  ⟹  R12 = (7/2)2/3 R22 = 2.305 R22

Substituting the two squared distances gives 65 + (y+2)2 = 2.305[20 + (y+7)2], which expands to

1.305y2 + 28.27y + 90.06 = 0  ⟹  y = −3.88 m  or  y = −17.78 m

The key prints −17.87 for the second root; that comes from rounding the discriminant to 330.9 before taking the square root. Carrying the coefficients above gives −17.78. Both points make Ez zero, but neither makes the whole field zero — the x and y components survive.

Three-minute check. Reproduce the lecture's two-charge Example 3.1: +20 nC at (1,0,0), −20 nC at (0,1,0), observation point (0,0,1). Each distance is √2, the two ẑ contributions cancel, and the surviving terms give 63.64(−x̂ + ŷ) V/m. If the cancellation surprises you, draw the two arrows.
What loses marks. Adding magnitudes instead of components; forgetting the 4πε0; writing ri − r for one charge and r − ri for the other; and answering "E = 0" when the question only set one component to zero.

3.3Line, surface and volume distributions

Real charge is never concentrated at a mathematical point. When the size of the charged body is comparable to the distance you care about, replace the sum over point charges by an integral over the body. The source coordinates get primes; the observation point stays unprimed. That single convention prevents most confusion.

DistributionDensityUnitElementTotal charge
LineρlC/mdq = ρl dl′∫L ρl dl′
SurfaceρsC/m2dq = ρs dS′∫S ρs dS′
VolumeρvC/m3dq = ρv dv′∫V ρv dv′

Each element contributes the point-charge field with dq in place of Q. The only new difficulty is that both the distance and the direction change from element to element, so they must stay inside the integral:

E = (1/4πε0) ∫ (r − r′) dq / |r − r′|3
dq = ρldl′ (line)  ·  ρsdS′ (surface)  ·  ρvdv′ (volume)
V = (1/4πε0) ∫ dq / |r − r′|

The potential integral is a scalar, so it is often the faster route: compute V first, then take E = −∇V if the field is also wanted.

One density, three geometriesSwitch line/surface/volume and compare the element and the units

A ring of charge, on its axis

Take a ring of radius a in the z = 0 plane, centre at the origin, uniform line density ρl. The observation point is (0, 0, z). A source element sits at r′ = a(cosφ′ x̂ + sinφ′ ŷ) = aρ̂′, with dl′ = a dφ′.

R = zẑ − aρ̂′,    |R| = √(a2 + z2)   (the same for every element)

The ρ̂′ components cancel in opposite pairs around the ring; only ẑ survives. Since dq = ρla dφ′ and ∫02π dφ′ = 2π, the total charge is Q = 2πaρl:

E = Qz ẑ / [4πε0 (a2 + z2)3/2]      V = Q / [4πε0 √(a2 + z2)]

Numbers: Q = 10 nC, a = 0.5 m, z = 1.2 m. Then √(a2+z2) = 1.3 m and

V = 9×109 × 10×10−9 / 1.3 = 69.2 V,    Ez = V z/(a2+z2) = 69.2 × 1.2/1.69 = 49.2 V/m

Three limits worth remembering: at z = 0 the field is zero by symmetry while the potential is maximum; at z ≫ a the ring looks like a point charge; and on the axis the field peaks at z = a/√2, not at the centre.

Semi-infinite line — lecture Example 3.2

A line from −∞ to 0 on the z-axis carries ρl = 100 nC/m. Find E at P(0, 0, 2).

Source: r′ = z′ẑ, dl′ = dz′. Observation: r = 2ẑ. Therefore R = (2 − z′)ẑ and, because z′ ≤ 0, the distance 2 − z′ is positive and equals its own modulus.

E = (ρl/4πε0) ∫−∞0 (2−z′) dz′/(2−z′)3 ẑ = (ρl/4πε0) ẑ ∫−∞0 dz′/(2−z′)2
= (ρl/4πε0) [1/(2−z′)]−∞0 ẑ = (ρl/4πε0) (1/2) ẑ

Substitute ρl = 100×10−9 and use 1/(4πε0) = 36π×109/4π = 9×109:

E = (100×10−9 × 9×109) × (1/2) ẑ = 450 ẑ V/m

A 1 nC charge at P feels F = qE = 10−9 × 450 = 450 nN in +ẑ. The field points away from the line because the line is positive and lies below the point.

Source discrepancy in the lecture. The problem statement says a 1 µC charge, but the worked solution substitutes 1 nC. The printed 450 nN belongs to 1 nC. For 1 µC the force would be 450 µN, a thousand times larger. In the control, underline the prefix before you compute.

Disk and annular disk, on the axis

A disk of radius a with uniform surface density ρs is a stack of rings. Put r′ for the ring radius, so dq = ρs2πr′dr′, and integrate the ring result:

Ez = (ρsz/2ε0) ∫0a r′dr′/(r′2+z2)3/2 = (ρs/2ε0) [1 − z/√(a2+z2)]   (z > 0)
V = (ρs/2ε0) [√(a2+z2) − |z|]

For an annulus with inner radius b, subtract the inner disk: Ez = (ρs/2ε0)[z/√(b2+z2) − z/√(a2+z2)]. Letting b → 0 recovers the disk; letting a → ∞ gives the infinite-sheet field ρs/2ε0.

Numbers: ρs = 10 nC/m2, a = 0.5 m, z = 1.2 m. The coefficient is ρs/2ε0 = 565.5 V/m, and 1 − 1.2/1.3 = 0.0769, so

Ez = 565.5 × 0.0769 = 43.5 V/m,    V = 565.5 × (1.3 − 1.2) = 56.5 V
Distribution mistakes that cost whole questions.
  • Wrong density. ρl is C/m, ρs is C/m2, ρv is C/m3. A surface integral with a line density is dimensionally impossible.
  • Missing Jacobian. dl′ = a dφ′ on a ring, dS′ = ρ′dρ′dφ′ on a disk, dv′ = r′2sinθ′dr′dθ′dφ′ in a sphere.
  • Primed/unprimed mixing. The integration variable is the source coordinate; the observation point is a constant during the integral.
  • Symmetry ignored. On the ring axis the transverse components cancel. If you integrate them anyway you will usually get zero after a long fight — say why they cancel instead.

3.4Electric flux and Gauss's law

Flux counts field lines crossing a surface. For a small patch, only the component normal to the patch contributes: dψ = D·dS = DndS. For a closed surface the outward normal is the convention, and the total outward flux is

ψ = ∮S D·dS = Qenc = ∫v ρv dv

This is Gauss's law. It repackages Coulomb's law into a statement about enclosed charge: the flux through any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it, not on the shape of the surface or on where the outside charges sit. Dividing by the volume and shrinking the surface gives the point form,

∇·D = ρv     with    D = ε0E  (free space)  or  D = εE  (medium)

D is convenient because it does not change when you change the dielectric: at fixed free charge, doubling ε halves E and leaves D alone. Its units, C/m2, are the same as surface charge density.

The symmetry recipe. Choose a surface on which D is either normal or parallel, and on which |D| is constant. Sphere for a point charge or spherical shell; coaxial cylinder for a line; pillbox for a sheet. Then ∮D·dS collapses to D × (area), and D falls out by division.
Flux through a closed surfaceSwitch the surface and move the charge inside or outside; watch Qenc and the flux

Key worked example — Control 2024–25, Exercise 2

In free space the volume density is ρv = (ρ0/a) r cosθ C/m3, with ρ0 and a constants. Find the total charge inside (a) the sphere r ≤ a, and (b) the cone r ≤ a, 0.9π ≤ θ ≤ π.

Set up the volume integral. In spherical coordinates dv = r2 sinθ dr dθ dφ. The density brings one more power of r, so the radial integrand is r3:

Q = ∫02π∫θ1θ2∫0a (ρ0/a) r cosθ · r2 sinθ dr dθ dφ
= (ρ0/a) (∫0a r3 dr) (∫θ1θ2 sinθ cosθ dθ) (∫02π dφ)

The three integrals separate because the limits are independent. The radial one is a4/4; the azimuthal one is 2π. For the polar one, substitute u = sinθ, du = cosθ dθ:

∫ sinθ cosθ dθ = ∫ u du = u2/2 = sin2θ/2

(a) Full sphere, θ from 0 to π.

Q = (ρ0/a)(a4/4)(2π)[½ sin2θ]0π = (ρ0a3/4)(2π)(0 − 0) = 0

The upper hemisphere carries positive charge (cosθ > 0) and the lower hemisphere carries exactly the opposite amount. The two halves cancel. Note that this is a statement about the total, not about the density: ρv is not zero anywhere except on the equator.

(b) Cone, θ from 0.9π to π. Now sin2(0.9π) does not vanish. Since sin(0.9π) = sin(0.1π) = 0.3090, its square is 0.09549:

Q = (ρ0a3/4)(2π)[(0 − 0.09549)/2] = −(ρ0a3π/4)(0.09549) = −0.0750 ρ0a3 C

The sign is worth a sentence in the exam: the cone lies below the equator (θ > π/2), where cosθ < 0, so the enclosed charge must be negative. The result 0.075 is exactly (π/4)sin2(0.1π): keep the exact form until the last line, then round.

Why this is the control's favourite integral. It tests four things at once: spherical dv, the r3 from ρv ∝ r, the sinθ substitution, and the geometry of the limits. Change any one and the answer changes completely.
Gauss-law mistakes.
  • ε versus ε0. In free space D = ε0E. In a dielectric use ε = εrε0; the flux of D is still Qenc, but E is reduced by εr.
  • Outside charges. They contribute to D at points on the surface but contribute zero net flux. Only Qenc counts.
  • Open surfaces. ∮ over a closed surface equals Qenc; ∫ over an open surface is just a flux and has no such shortcut.
  • Missing Jacobian. The r2 sinθ is not optional, and the θ limits for a cone are [0.9π, π], not [0, 0.9π].
  • Sphere is a volume, not a shell. "Inside the sphere r = a" means r ≤ a; a surface integral would give a different quantity.
One-line check. A 10 nC point charge at the centre of a 2 m-radius sphere: D = Q/(4πr2) = 10×10−9/(16π) = 1.99×10−10 C/m2, and E = D/ε0 = 22.5 V/m. The flux is 10 nC regardless of the radius.

3.5Electric scalar potential

Voltage is work per unit charge. Move a positive test charge from B to A against the field: you do positive work, the field does negative work, and VA − VB is your work per unit charge. The definition that survives every problem is the line integral:

VAB = VA − VB = −∫BA E·dl   (V)

The minus sign is the whole physics: walking along E takes you downhill, so V decreases and the integral of E·dl is positive while VA − VB is negative. For a closed path, A = B and ∮E·dl = 0: electrostatic fields are conservative.

In differential form the same statement is

E = −∇V = −(∂V/∂x x̂ + ∂V/∂y ŷ + ∂V/∂z ẑ)

For a point charge at the origin, integrating from R to infinity gives the absolute potential V = q/(4πε0R), with the zero at infinity. Surfaces of constant V are equipotentials — spheres around a point charge. E is perpendicular to them and points toward decreasing V.

Potential map of a point chargeMove the probe: compare V, |E|, and the value a step downhill

Worked example — potential from a given field

Let E = 6xy x̂ + 3x2 ŷ V/m, with V = 0 at the origin. Find V(2, 1, 0).

1. Check that the field is conservative. The only non-zero curl component is the z one: ∂Ey/∂x − ∂Ex/∂y = 6x − 6x = 0. So a potential exists and the path does not matter.

2. Integrate along a convenient path. Go from (0,0,0) to (x,0,0), where E·dl = 0 because y = 0; then from (x,0,0) to (x,y,0), where dl = dy ŷ and Ey = 3x2:

V(x,y) = −∫0y 3x2 dy′ = −3x2y

3. Evaluate and check. At (2, 1, 0): V = −3(4)(1) = −12 V. Differentiating back gives −∂V/∂x = 6xy and −∂V/∂y = 3x2, which is the given field. At that point E = (12, 12, 0) V/m, so |E| = 17.0 V/m. A positive charge there is pushed toward increasing x and y, where V is lower.

Potential of a ring and a disk

Superposition works for V exactly as for E, except that the sum is scalar. For the ring of the previous section, every element is at the same distance √(a2+z2):

ObjectV on the axisSame point: radius 0.5 m, height 1.2 m
Ring, total charge QQ/[4πε0√(a2+z2)]69.2 V
Disk, density ρs(ρs/2ε0)[√(a2+z2) − |z|]56.5 V for ρs = 10 nC/m2

The ring result also follows from V = Q/(4πε0R) with R = √(a2+z2), a useful check: every part of the ring is equally far from the axis point. Then Ez = −dV/dz must return the ring field, and it does: 49.2 V/m at the same point.

A quick absolute-potential calibration: a 1 nC charge gives 9.0 V at 1 m, 4.5 V at 2 m and 3.0 V at 3 m. The potential difference between 1 m and 3 m is 6.0 V; the field integral −∫31(9/r2)dr = 9(1 − 1/3) = 6.0 V agrees.

Potential mistakes.
  • Sign of VAB. It is VA − VB, not VB − VA. If you instead compute ∫AB, the sign reverses.
  • Dropping the minus in E = −∇V. Without it the field points uphill, which is impossible for electrostatics.
  • "V = 0 means E = 0". False. On the midplane of a dipole V = 0 by cancellation, but E points from the positive to the negative charge and is not zero.
  • Reference point. V = q/(4πε0R) sets V(∞) = 0. An infinite line or sheet cannot use infinity as its zero; only potential differences are meaningful there.
  • Scalar does not mean unsigned. Add potentials with the sign of each source charge, then take the gradient once. Do not take |V|.

3.6Check yourself

Three questions, one per core skill. Answer before opening the solution.

Coulomb · force

A +2 nC charge sits at the origin and a −2 nC charge at (0, 0, 3) m. What is the force on the negative charge?

Show answer
F = 9×109(2×10−9)(2×10−9)/32 = 36×10−9/9 = 4×10−9 N. Opposite signs attract, so the force on the charge at z = 3 points toward the origin, i.e. along −ẑ.
Volume integral

A sphere of radius a carries ρv = ρ0r/a for r ≤ a. What is the total charge?

Show answer
Q = (ρ0/a)∫0ar·r2dr × 4π = (ρ0/a)(a4/4)(4π) = πρ0a3. The angular integrals give 4π; the extra power of r comes from the density.
Potential · sign

E = 6xy x̂ + 3x2 ŷ V/m and V(0,0,0) = 0. What is V at (2, 1, 0)?

Show answer
V = −∫E·dl = −3x2y. At (2,1,0) this is −3(4)(1) = −12 V. Check: −∂V/∂x = 6xy and −∂V/∂y = 3x2, which matches E.
