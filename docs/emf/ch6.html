Chapter 6 · Time-varying fields

Uniform plane waves

A uniform plane wave is what Maxwell’s equations look like when nothing depends on x or y: one direction of travel, E and H perpendicular to it and to each other, and a single ratio η that ties their amplitudes together. The 2020 final opens with exactly this object, so this chapter is built around one exam skill — read a phasor, extract β, λ, εr and η, then rebuild the fields.

Notation. Vectors are bold (E, H, k); phasors carry the subscript s (Es). η is the intrinsic impedance, not a coordinate.

The exam contract. From Hs = H0 e−jβ·r you must be able to do five moves: (1) read β and the direction k̂; (2) λ = 2π/β; (3) n = βc/ω, then εr = n²/μr; (4) η = η0√(μr/εr); (5) Es = η Hs × k̂. The wave exercises in the 2020 final are variations on those five lines. Drill the pattern →

6.1Free space: the wave Maxwell predicted

Set ρ = 0 and J = 0. Faraday and Ampère become two coupled curls:

∇ × E = −μ0 ∂H/∂t     ∇ × H = ε0 ∂E/∂t

Take the curl of Faraday’s law and substitute Ampère’s law into the right-hand side:

∇ × ∇ × E = −μ0 ∂/∂t (∇ × H) = −μ0ε0 ∂²E/∂t²
∇(∇·E) − ∇²E = −μ0ε0 ∂²E/∂t²
∇²E = μ0ε0 ∂²E/∂t²     because ∇·E = 0

That is the wave equation: the Laplacian of E at a point is proportional to its acceleration in time. A shape that satisfies it travels without changing shape, at the speed fixed by the two constants. For a sinusoid, replace ∂/∂t by jω and ∂²/∂t² by −ω². The result is the vector Helmholtz equation:

∇²Es + k0² Es = 0
k0 = ω√(μ0ε0) = ω/c

What k0 means. It is the phase constant: the number of radians of phase the wave accumulates per metre of travel. It is not a spring constant, and it is not 1/λ — one wavelength contains 2π radians, which is where the 2π in λ = 2π/k0 comes from.

Uniform means the fields do not depend on x or y, only on z. Then ∂²/∂x² = ∂²/∂y² = 0, and the vector equation collapses to a scalar one:

d²Exs/dz² + k0² Exs = 0
⟹   Exs = Ex0 e−jk0z

Reinsert ejωt and take the real part. The forward-travelling solution is a cosine whose argument is ωt − k0z:

Ex = Ex0 cos(ωt − k0z)
Hy = (Ex0/η0) cos(ωt − k0z)
η0 = √(μ0/ε0) = 120π ≈ 377 Ω

H comes from Faraday’s law, not from a separate guess: −jk0Ex0e−jk0z = −jωμ0Hys, so Hys = (k0/ωμ0)Exs = Exs/η0. The electric and magnetic fields are in phase in a lossless medium; their ratio is the real constant η0.

The animation labels the magnetic field B rather than H. In a nonmagnetic medium B = μ0H, so B has the same phase and the same waveform as H; only its amplitude scale differs.

λ = 2π/k0 = c/f     vp = ω/k0 = 1/√(μ0ε0) = c     T = 1/f

Why the crest moves. Track a crest by holding the phase constant: ωt − k0z = constant. Differentiate with respect to t: ω − k0(dz/dt) = 0, so dz/dt = ω/k0. The pattern shifts by one λ every T, which is the same statement.

The triad. E is along x, H is along y, and E × H points along +z — the direction of propagation. Reverse either field and the wave travels the other way. In a plane wave E, H and k̂ form a right-handed orthogonal set, which is the whole content of Es = η Hs × k̂.

Worked example 6.1 — the 2020 final, recomputed

Given. A uniform plane wave in a medium with μr = 3 has

Hs = 30 e−j36πx ẑ  mA/m

The paper’s header reads f = 300 MHz. Find β, λ, εr and η, then rebuild E.

1 · Read β and the direction. The exponent is −j36πx, so β = 36π rad/m and k̂ = +x̂. No computation yet; this is the single most common place to lose marks by “finding” β from f when it is already printed in the exponent.

2 · Wavelength, always from β.

λ = 2π/β = 2π/(36π) = 1/18 m = 0.05556 m = 5.56 cm

3 · Speed and refractive index. The official solution substitutes ω = 2π(900 MHz), so do that first:

ω = 2π × 9 × 108 = 5.655 × 109 rad/s
vp = ω/β = 5.655 × 109 / (36π) = 5.0 × 107 m/s
n = c/vp = 3 × 108 / 5.0 × 107 = 6

4 · Permittivity. For a nonmagnetic-looking problem the trap is forgetting that n² = μrεr, not εr alone:

μrεr = n² = 36  ⟹  εr = 36/3 = 12

5 · Intrinsic impedance.

η = η0√(μr/εr) = 377√(3/12) = 377 × 0.5 = 188.5 Ω

6 · Rebuild E. H is along ẑ and k̂ is along x̂, so ẑ × x̂ = ŷ:

Es = η Hs × k̂ = 188.5 × 0.030 ŷ = 5.66 e−j36πx ŷ  V/m

In the time domain: Ey(x,t) = 5.66 cos(ωt − 36πx) V/m, with ω = 5.655 × 109 rad/s. E is along y, H is along z, and E × H points along +x — consistent with k̂.

300 MHz or 900 MHz? This is the arithmetic to correct. The scan’s header says 300 MHz, but the official line divides by 9 × 108 and every marked answer (εr = 12, η = 188.5 Ω, λ = 5.56 cm) is the 900 MHz set. The next page of the same solution even writes ω = 2π × 900 MHz. At the printed 300 MHz: ω = 1.885 × 109 rad/s, vp = 1.667 × 107 m/s, n = 18, εr = 324/3 = 108, η = 377√(3/108) = 62.8 Ω. λ is still 5.56 cm because β is given independently. Keeping f = 300 MHz and forcing εr = 12 would instead give β = 12π and λ = 16.7 cm; that set cannot produce the key’s 5.56 cm either.

60-second drill

At f = 150 MHz in free space, a wave has Ex0 = 50 V/m. Then ω = 2π(1.5 × 108) = 9.425 × 108 rad/s, k0 = ω/c = π rad/m, λ = 2π/π = 2 m, T = 1/f = 6.67 ns, Hy0 = 50/377 = 0.133 A/m. If you can produce that chain in under a minute, the free-space part is done.

Common mistakes — free space.
  • rad/s versus Hz. k = ω√(με) needs ω. At 300 MHz, ω = 1.885 × 109 rad/s and k0 = 2π rad/m. Substituting f = 300 × 106 in place of ω gives k0 = 1 rad/m and λ = 6.28 m — wrong by a factor of 2π.
  • λ = 2π/β, not 1/β. β = 36π gives λ = 5.56 cm, not 1/(36π) = 8.84 mm.
  • η is an amplitude ratio. E0 = ηH0. If you need power density, S = ½ Re(E × H*) = E0²/(2η).
  • Do not mix 377 and 120π mid-chain. Pick η0 = 120π ≈ 376.99 Ω and keep it; rounding to 377 changes the third digit only.

6.2Dielectrics: same wave, slower and shorter

A perfect dielectric is free space with two dials: ε = ε0εr and μ = μ0μr. Maxwell’s equations do not change; only the constants do. The Helmholtz equation becomes ∇²Es + k²Es = 0 with

k = ω√(με) = k0√(εrμr)
η = √(μ/ε) = η0√(μr/εr)
vp = 1/√(με) = c/n     λ = 2π/k = λ0/n     n = √(εrμr)

Intuition. The medium stores more energy per unit field, so the wave takes longer to hand the energy along: it slows down and its wavelength shrinks. The frequency does not change — the source sets it before the wave enters the medium. Since v = fλ, a smaller v at fixed f means a smaller λ. Everything scales by the single number n.

Most nonmagnetic media have μr ≈ 1, so n ≈ √εr. Glass with εr = 4 has n = 2: half the speed, half the wavelength. Water with εr = 81 has n = 9: one ninth of c.

Real dielectrics are not perfect. A small conductivity adds loss, measured by the loss tangent tan δ = σ/(ωε) = ε″/ε′. Below 0.01 the medium is low-loss and the formulas above are accurate enough; above 100 it behaves as a good conductor (§6.3). The symbol δ is overloaded: here it is the loss angle, in §6.3 it is the skin depth.

Worked example 6.2 — polyethylene at 9.375 GHz

Given. f = 9.375 GHz, εr = 2.26, μr = 1, E0 = 500 V/m. Find β, λ, vp, η and H0.

1 · Refractive index.

n = √(2.26 × 1) = 1.5033

2 · Phase constant. Use either k = k0n or k = ω/v:

k0 = 2πf/c = 2π(9.375 × 109)/(3 × 108) = 196.35 rad/m
β = k0n = 196.35 × 1.5033 = 295.2 rad/m

The lecture key writes “295 rad/s”. The number is right; the unit must be rad/m.

3 · Wavelength and speed.

λ = 2π/β = 2π/295.2 = 0.02129 m = 2.13 cm
vp = c/n = 3 × 108/1.5033 = 1.996 × 108 m/s

4 · Impedance.

η = η0/n = 376.99/1.5033 = 250.8 Ω

5 · Magnetic amplitude.

H0 = E0/η = 500/250.8 = 1.994 A/m

Check. λ = v/f = 1.996 × 108/9.375 × 109 = 2.13 cm. Both routes agree, so the 2π was not lost.

Quick drill — 300 MHz in εr = 4

n = 2, v = 1.5 × 108 m/s, β = ω/v = (2π × 3 × 108)/(1.5 × 108) = 4π rad/m, λ = 0.5 m, η = 377/2 = 188.5 Ω. This is the same impedance as the 2020 final, but for a completely different reason: here μr = 1 and εr = 4; there μr = 3 and εr = 12. Both have μr/εr = 1/4.

Common mistakes — dielectrics.
  • k versus β. In a lossless dielectric k is real and equals β. In a conductor k is complex: with the course convention jk = α + jβ, k = β − jα. So β is the real part of k, and α is minus its imaginary part. Writing k = α + jβ instead of jk = α + jβ flips the sign of the attenuation.
  • εr versus ε. εr = 2.26 is dimensionless; ε = 2.26ε0 = 2.00 × 10−11 F/m. Substitute the second into √(με), never the first.
  • Missing square root. η = η0√(μr/εr), not η0(μr/εr). For εr = 4 the answer is 188.5 Ω, not 94.2 Ω.
  • Frequency is constant across the boundary. v and λ change by 1/n; f does not.
  • n = √(εrμr), not εrμr. For the 2020 final, n² = 36, so n = 6.

6.3Conductors: the wave dies by e−αz

A conductor is a dielectric with a leakage current J = σE. In phasor form, Ampère’s law is

∇ × Hs = (σ + jωε)Es = jω(ε − jσ/ω)Es

The real part σ is conduction current; the imaginary part ωε is displacement current. Their ratio decides which one dominates, and therefore which approximation you are allowed to use:

tan δ = σ/(ωε) = ε″/ε′
low-loss dielectric: tan δ < 0.01     good conductor: tan δ ≫ 1 (the course uses > 100)

Why the wave decays. The conduction current heats the medium, so energy leaves the wave. Mathematically the propagation constant becomes complex. Write jk = α + jβ; then

Ex = Ex0 e−αz cos(ωt − βz)

α is the attenuation constant in Np/m, β is the phase constant in rad/m. For a good conductor they turn out to be equal, and both are the reciprocal of the skin depth.

Derivation in one line. With σ ≫ ωε, k² ≈ −jωμσ. Since √(−j) = (1 − j)/√2, k = (1 − j)√(ωμσ/2), hence jk = (1 + j)√(ωμσ/2) = (1 + j)√(πfμσ). Therefore

α = β = √(πfμσ)
δ = 1/α = 1/√(πfμσ)
η = (1 + j)√(ωμ/2σ) = (1 + j)/(σδ)
|η| = √(ωμ/σ) = √2/(σδ)     ∠η = 45°

Skin depth. At z = δ the field is e−1 = 36.8% of its surface value; at 3δ it is 5.0%; at 5δ it is 0.67%, i.e. below 1%. That is why a conductor looks like a surface to a high-frequency wave: almost all the current is in the first few δ.

1 Np = 20 log10(e) dB = 8.686 dB

Because αδ = 1, every good conductor loses 8.686 dB of field amplitude per skin depth, whatever the material or frequency. That constant is worth memorising; it turns a δ calculation into a dB budget instantly. Power decays as e−2αz, so it loses 17.37 dB per δ.

Worked example 6.3 — sea water at five frequencies

Given. σ = 4 S/m, εr = 80, μr = 1. Find δ and the depth at which the field falls to 10−3 of its surface value.

1 · Check the good-conductor condition at 1 kHz.

σ/(ωε) = 4/(2π × 103 × 80 × 8.854 × 10−12) = 4/(4.45 × 10−6) = 8.99 × 105

Far greater than 1, so the approximation is excellent.

2 · Skin depth at 1 kHz.

πfμσ = π(103)(4π × 10−7)(4) = 1.579 × 10−2
δ = 1/√(1.579 × 10−2) = 1/0.1257 = 7.96 m

3 · Attenuation and the 10−3 depth.

α = 1/δ = 0.1257 Np/m    (×8.686 = 1.09 dB/m)
d = δ ln(103) = 7.96 × 6.908 = 55.0 m

4 · Repeat at higher f. δ ∝ 1/√f, so each ×100 in frequency divides δ by 10:

One rigor check. At 10 MHz the loss tangent is σ/(ωε) = 90, just below the course’s “> 100” rule. The last row is therefore an estimate, but 90 is still large enough that the good-conductor formulas are within a few percent. At 1 GHz the same water has σ/(ωε) ≈ 0.9 and the approximation fails completely — that is why the frequency slider flags it.

Impedance check at 1 MHz. |η| = √(ωμ/σ) = √(2π × 106 × 4π × 10−7/4) = 1.405 Ω, so η = 0.993 + j0.993 Ω. It is complex, with a 45° angle — not a real 1.4 Ω.

Worked example 6.4 — copper at 60 Hz and 1 MHz

Given. σ = 5.8 × 107 S/m, μr = 1.

60 Hz:   δ = 1/√(π × 60 × 4π × 10−7 × 5.8 × 107) = 8.53 mm
1 MHz:   δ = 8.53 mm/√(106/60) = 66.1 µm

Attenuation. α = 1/δ: 117 Np/m at 60 Hz, 1.51 × 104 Np/m at 1 MHz. In dB: 1.02 dB/mm at 60 Hz, 131 dB/mm at 1 MHz.

Impedance at 1 MHz. 1/(σδ) = 1/(5.8 × 107 × 6.61 × 10−5) = 2.61 × 10−4 Ω, so η = (1 + j)2.61 × 10−4 Ω, magnitude 3.69 × 10−4 Ω. Tiny, as a good conductor should be.

Why the two frequencies differ. δ ∝ f−1/2: a 16,667-fold increase in f shrinks δ by √16,667 ≈ 129. The 1 MHz wave lives in a layer thinner than a human hair, which is why RF current hugs the surface.

Common mistakes — conductors.
  • The square root in δ. δ = 1/√(πfμσ), not 1/(πfμσ). Doubling f shrinks δ by √2, not by 2.
  • η is complex, not real. η = (1 + j)/(σδ) has magnitude √2/(σδ) and angle +45°. Its real part is |η|/√2. Writing “η = 1.4 Ω” for sea water at 1 MHz loses the phase.
  • α = β only for a good conductor. Do not carry that equality back to a low-loss dielectric, where α ≈ 0 and β = ω√(με).
  • Np versus dB. e−1 = 0.368 is −8.686 dB, not −1 dB. If a problem asks for dB, multiply α by 8.686.
  • εr does not belong in the good-conductor formula. δ = 1/√(πfμσ) contains σ and μ only. That is not a mistake in the formula; it is the physics — the displacement current is negligible.
  • Check the approximation. At 1 GHz, sea water has σ/(ωε) ≈ 0.9, so it is no longer a good conductor and the simple δ formula is only indicative.

6.4Polarization: the path of the E tip

Polarization is the curve traced by the tip of E at a fixed z as time runs. For a wave travelling along +z, allow both transverse components and a phase on each:

Es = (Ex0ejφx x̂ + Ey0ejφy ŷ) e−jkz
Ex = Ex0 cos(ωt − kz + φx)    Ey = Ey0 cos(ωt − kz + φy)

Only the difference Δφ = φy − φx changes the shape. Adding the same phase to both components merely shifts the whole pattern in time.

Phase differenceAmplitudesPolarization
0° or 180°anyLinear. The tip stays on a straight line; the slope is ±Ey0/Ex0.
+90°Ex0 = Ey0Left-hand circular (IEEE, ejωt: E × ∂E/∂t is opposite k).
−90°Ex0 = Ey0Right-hand circular (same convention: E × ∂E/∂t along k).
±90°unequalEllipse with its axes along x and y.
anything elseanyEllipse tilted relative to the axes.

How to identify it from the phasor in ten seconds.

  1. Write both coefficients in polar form. If one component is missing, the wave is linear along the other axis.
  2. Compare the magnitudes. Equal magnitudes are necessary for circular, but not sufficient.
  3. Compare the phases. 0 or 180° means linear; ±90° with equal magnitudes means circular; everything else is elliptical.

Handedness, stated once. We use ejωt. The unambiguous test is the rotation vector E × ∂E/∂t: if it points along +k, the wave is right-hand circular; if it points along −k, left-hand. For Δφ = +90°, the tip starts at +x and moves toward −y, so E × ∂E/∂t points along −z: left-hand. Δφ = −90° gives right-hand. Textbooks that use e−jωt reverse the label, so state your convention. If the question only asks linear/circular/elliptical, handedness is a bonus, not the main answer.

Worked example 6.5 — read the ellipse from the phasor

Given. Es = (3x̂ + j4ŷ)e−jkz V/m, travelling along +z.

1 · Identify the coefficients. Ex0 = 3, φx = 0; Ey0 = 4, φy = +90°. Magnitudes are unequal, so the wave cannot be circular.

2 · Classify. Δφ = +90° with unequal amplitudes → elliptical, with axes along x and y. At z = 0:

Ex = 3 cos(ωt)    Ey = 4 cos(ωt + 90°) = −4 sin(ωt)
(Ex/3)² + (Ey/4)² = cos² + sin² = 1

The tip traces an ellipse with semi-axes 3 and 4 V/m. Axial ratio = 4/3 = 1.33.

3 · Handedness. At t = 0, E = +3x̂. A moment later Ey is negative, so the tip moves toward −y. Then E × ∂E/∂t points along −z, opposite to k, so this is left-hand elliptical (IEEE).

4 · Compare the special cases. 3x̂ + 3ŷ would be linear at 45° (Δφ = 0). 3x̂ + j3ŷ would be a circle of radius 3 (equal amplitudes, +90°) — left-hand circular. The j alone does not make a wave circular; the magnitudes must match too.

Common mistakes — polarization.
  • “Two components” does not mean elliptical. Equal amplitudes with Δφ = 0 is linear, no matter how many components are written.
  • Magnitude ratio alone is not enough. Circular needs equal magnitudes and ±90°. Either condition alone gives an ellipse or a line.
  • Handedness is convention-dependent. Under e−jωt the same phasor gets the opposite label. State your time convention, or use the rotation vector E × ∂E/∂t, which removes the ambiguity.
  • j means 90°, not “imaginary field”. A factor j on one component is a quarter-cycle time shift.
  • Polarization is about E. H is perpendicular to E and traces the same ellipse rotated by 90° in the transverse plane; do not classify from H and then attach it to E without checking the triad.

6.5Check yourself

Three questions, one per exam move. Answer first, then open the solution.

Q1 · dielectric
A 300 MHz uniform plane wave travels in a medium with μr = 1 and εr = 4. Which set is correct?
Show answer
n = √4 = 2, so v = c/n = 1.5 × 108 m/s and β = ω/v = (2π × 3 × 108)/(1.5 × 108) = 4π rad/m. Then λ = 2π/β = 0.5 m. b. Option (c) divides λ by n twice; option (d) uses k0 instead of k.
Q2 · conductor
Copper has σ = 5.8 × 107 S/m and μr = 1. What is its skin depth at 1 MHz?
Show answer
δ = 1/√(πfμσ) = 1/√(π × 106 × 4π × 10−7 × 5.8 × 107) = 1/1.513 × 104 = 6.61 × 10−5 m = 66 µm. a. A factor-of-10 slip gives 6.6 µm or 660 µm; 47 µm comes from putting 2π under the root instead of π.
Q3 · polarization
Es = (5x̂ + j5ŷ)e−jkz V/m, travelling along +z. Using ejωt, what is the polarization?
Show answer
Equal magnitudes (5 and 5) and Δφ = φy − φx = +90°. Equal amplitudes plus a quarter-cycle phase difference makes the tip trace a circle, so the wave is circular. c. If the amplitudes differed, the same 90° would give an ellipse; if Δφ were 0° or 180°, it would be a straight line. Under the stated ejωt convention this particular +90° case is left-hand circular, but the exam’s classification is simply “circular”.
