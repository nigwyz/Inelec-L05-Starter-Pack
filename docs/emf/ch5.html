Maxwell's equations

Four equations turn charges and currents into fields, and fields back into charges and currents. This chapter is the toolkit for the final's short-question block: recognize a law from its integral or differential form, compute the flux or circulation, and get the sign right. Every section ends with the arithmetic the exam expects, not just the formula.

5.0The four laws at a glance

Click a card. The diagram shows what the law measures; the text gives the plain-language version and the form you should be able to write from memory. The four are not independent facts. They are one system: charge is the source of D, there is no magnetic charge, a changing B circulates E, and current plus a changing D circulates H.

5.1Faraday's law: a changing flux drives a loop

Faraday found that a loop can be driven without a battery. All that is required is a magnetic flux through the loop that changes with time. The induced emf is the work per unit charge done around the loop. It is not a force, despite the name; the unit is the volt.

∮C E · dl = − d/dt ∫S B · dS

For a loop fixed in space, the derivative moves inside the integral and becomes a partial derivative of B:

∮C E · dl = − ∫S ∂B/∂t · dS   ⇔   ∇ × E = − ∂B/∂t

Stokes' theorem turns the left side into ∫S(∇ × E) · dS. Both sides are now surface integrals over the same arbitrary surface, so the integrands must match. That is the differential form.

Orientation is part of the equation. Curl the fingers of your right hand along the contour C; the thumb points along the positive normal dS. Reverse either the contour or the surface normal and both sides change sign, so the law still holds. Pick one orientation at the start and keep it to the end of the problem.

Lenz's law in one sentence

The induced current appears in the direction that opposes the change in flux. If the flux is increasing, the induced current creates flux in the opposite direction. If the flux is decreasing, it tries to maintain the original flux. This is the meaning of the minus sign, and it is also the fastest way to get the direction without doing a signed integral.

  1. Choose C and its normal by the right-hand rule.
  2. Compute ΦB = ∫B · dS with that normal, including its sign.
  3. Compute emf = −dΦB/dt. A negative result means the current runs opposite to your chosen C.
Magnet and coilslide the velocity through zero; watch the current reverse

Example 5.1 — a circular loop in a time-varying field

A circular loop of radius a = 5.0 cm lies in the xy-plane. A uniform field B = B0 cos(ωt) ẑ passes through it, with B0 = 0.20 T and f = 50 Hz. Find the flux, the emf at t = 5.0 ms, and the direction of the induced current.

A = πa² = π(0.050)² = 7.854 × 10−3 m²
ΦB = B0A cos ωt = 0.20 × 7.854 × 10−3 cos ωt = 1.571 × 10−3 cos ωt Wb
ω = 2πf = 2π(50) = 314.2 rad/s
emf = −dΦB/dt = ωB0A sin ωt = 0.4935 sin ωt V

At t = 5.0 ms, ωt = 314.2 × 5.0 × 10−3 = 1.571 rad = π/2, so sin ωt = 1 and emf = 0.494 V. At that instant B is positive but falling through zero, so the upward flux is decreasing. The induced current must produce +ẑ, which means counter-clockwise seen from +z.

Check with the differential form: for ρ < a, Eφ = (ρ/2)ωB0 sin ωt. At ρ = a = 0.050 m, Eφ = 1.57 V/m, and 2πaEφ = 0.494 V. At t = 0 the field is maximum but the emf is zero, because the flux is momentarily not changing.

Example 5.2 — the moving rod

A rod of length l = 0.40 m slides at v = 2.0 m/s along two frictionless rails in a uniform field B = 0.50 T pointing out of the page (+ẑ). The rails are joined by R = 2.0 Ω. Find the emf, the current, the magnetic force on the rod, and check the power balance.

emf = Blv = 0.50 × 0.40 × 2.0 = 0.40 V
I = emf/R = 0.40/2.0 = 0.20 A
F = IlB = 0.20 × 0.40 × 0.50 = 0.040 N, opposite to v
Pmech = Fv = 0.040 × 2.0 = 0.080 W
Pelec = I²R = (0.20)² × 2.0 = 0.080 W ✓

Direction. The charge velocity is +x̂ and B is +ẑ, so v × B = x̂ × ẑ = −ŷ. Positive charges are pushed down the rod, so the current runs down the rod. The loop area is growing, so the outward flux is increasing; the induced current must produce inward flux (−ẑ), which is clockwise in the plane. The magnetic force on the rod is then −x̂: it opposes the motion. Lenz's law and the force calculation agree.

Common mistake: dropping the minus sign. The minus is not decoration. If you compute |emf| = |dΦ/dt| and then guess the direction, you will be wrong half the time. Either keep the signed normal consistently, or compute the magnitude and use Lenz explicitly. Also note that emf is maximum when the flux is zero and changing fastest, not when B is maximum. In Example 5.1, B is maximum at t = 0 but emf = 0.
Common mistake: partial versus total derivative. For a loop fixed in space, d/dt and ∂/∂t are interchangeable inside the flux integral. For a moving loop they are not. The universal law is emf = −dΦB/dt, with the total derivative. The differential law ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t describes the electric field at a fixed point; it does not contain the motional term v × B. In Example 5.2 the field is static, so ∂B/∂t = 0, yet the emf is 0.40 V. Do not “prove” the rod's emf from the partial derivative.
Exam recipe for a Faraday question. (1) Write the flux through the loop with the given field. (2) Differentiate it; if the loop moves, the area is time-dependent. (3) The magnitude is |dΦ/dt|; the direction comes from Lenz. (4) If the question asks for the induced electric field, use symmetry: a circular path gives Eφ = emf/(2πρ).

5.2Ampère–Maxwell: the displacement current

Ampère's law works beautifully for steady currents. Put a capacitor in the circuit and it contradicts itself. Take a contour C around the wire feeding one plate. Surface S1 cuts the wire, so the enclosed current is i(t). Surface S2 balloons through the capacitor gap, so the enclosed current is zero. Both surfaces have the same boundary C, but they give different values of ∮H · dl. Ampère's law is incomplete whenever the current is time-varying.

Maxwell's fix was to add a current that exists only while the electric field changes. Between the plates there is no charge crossing the gap, but there is a changing electric flux. He called it the displacement current:

∮C H · dl = ∫S Jc · dS + ∫S ∂D/∂t · dS
∇ × H = Jc + ∂D/∂t

Jc = σE is conduction current: charge actually moving. Jd = ∂D/∂t is displacement current density: a changing electric flux. Both have units of A/m². The displacement term is not a flow of charge; it is the piece of the magnetic source that exists in empty space, and it is what allows electromagnetic waves to propagate.

Charging capacitorslide dV/dt negative; the displacement arrow reverses

Example 5.3 — displacement current in a parallel-plate capacitor

Parallel plates of radius a = 10 cm are separated by d = 2.0 mm. The voltage is V(t) = V0 sin ωt, with V0 = 200 V and f = 100 kHz. (a) Find the amplitude of the displacement current. (b) At the instant V = 120 V and dV/dt = +5.0 × 105 V/s, find Id, Jd, and the magnetic field at the plate rim. (c) Check the result with ε0 dΦE/dt.

A = π(0.10)² = 3.142 × 10−2 m²
C = ε0A/d = (8.854 × 10−12)(3.142 × 10−2)/(2.0 × 10−3) = 1.391 × 10−10 F = 139 pF
E = V/d,   ΦE = EA = VA/d
Id = ε0 dΦE/dt = (ε0A/d) dV/dt = C dV/dt

(a) ω = 2π(105) = 6.283 × 105 rad/s. The derivative dV/dt = V0ω cos ωt has amplitude V0ω, so

Id,max = C V0ω = (1.391 × 10−10)(200)(6.283 × 105) = 1.748 × 10−2 A = 17.5 mA

(b) With dV/dt = 5.0 × 105 V/s,

Id = (1.391 × 10−10)(5.0 × 105) = 6.95 × 10−5 A = 69.5 µA
Jd = Id/A = 6.95 × 10−5 / 3.142 × 10−2 = 2.21 × 10−3 A/m²
E = 120/(2.0 × 10−3) = 6.0 × 104 V/m
ε0 ∂E/∂t = (8.854 × 10−12)(5.0 × 105/(2.0 × 10−3)) = 2.21 × 10−3 A/m² ✓

(c) Take a circular Amperian loop of radius ρ = a between the plates. By symmetry, H is azimuthal and constant on the loop, so 2πaH = Id:

H = Id/(2πa) = 6.95 × 10−5/(2π × 0.10) = 1.11 × 10−4 A/m
B = μ0H = (4π × 10−7)(1.11 × 10−4) = 1.39 × 10−10 T

The displacement current points from the positive plate to the negative plate, the same direction as the conduction current in the wire. The plates are charging, so E grows from + to −, and Jd = ∂D/∂t points the same way.

Common mistake: the direction of the displacement current. Jd = ∂D/∂t follows the direction in which D is increasing, not necessarily the direction of D itself. On discharge, D still points from the + plate to the − plate, but its magnitude is falling, so Jd points the other way and the wire current reverses too. Draw the arrows from the sign of dV/dt, not from the sign of V.
Who wins, conduction or displacement? The ratio |Jc|/|Jd| = σ/(ωε) is the loss tangent. In a good conductor it is large, so the conduction term dominates and the wave attenuates quickly. In a good dielectric at high frequency it is small, so the displacement term dominates and the wave travels. Chapter 6 turns this competition into skin depth and the loss tangent.
Exam link. Final 2024–25, Exercise 2 gives H and asks for E. The move is exactly this section: ∇ × H = ∂D/∂t in a region with no conduction current, then integrate with respect to time. Q4 of the same paper asks you to compute the curl in cylindrical coordinates; do not expand the determinant in Cartesian coordinates by habit.

5.3Gauss's laws: where fields begin and end

Gauss's two laws are the statements about sources. The electric law counts charge. The magnetic law says the count is always zero.

∮S D · dS = ∫V ρv dV = Qenc   ⇔   ∇ · D = ρv
∮S B · dS = 0   ⇔   ∇ · B = 0

Read the electric law first. The surface is closed and the normal points outward. Qenc is the net charge inside that surface, not the total charge in the universe. At a point where ρv is positive, D lines start; where ρv is negative, they end; where ρv = 0, they pass through. The differential form is the same statement at a point, and it is the one you use when a problem gives you D and asks for the charge density.

Read the magnetic law second. There is no magnetic charge. Every B line that enters a closed surface must leave it, so the net flux is zero. The lines do not begin or end; they close on themselves. A bar magnet is a dipole, not two monopoles: the lines leave the north face, curve around, and enter the south face. If you draw a closed surface around the whole magnet, the flux out near the north pole is exactly cancelled by the flux in near the south pole.

SymmetryGaussian surfaceResult you can use
Point charge or spherical shellSphere of radius rD = Q/(4πr²) r̂
Infinite line chargeCoaxial cylinder of radius ρ and length LD = ρl/(2πρ) ρ̂
Infinite sheet of chargePillbox straddling the sheet|D| = ρs/2 on each side

Gauss's law is always true. It is only a calculating tool when the symmetry lets you pull D or B out of the integral. If the symmetry is absent, use Coulomb or Biot–Savart, or the differential form; do not invent a constant field.

Example 5.4 — a point charge and a closed surface

A charge Q = 2.0 nC sits at the origin. Find D and E at r = 0.30 m, and check the D-flux through a sphere of that radius.

D = Q/(4πr²) = 2.0 × 10−9/(4π × 0.0900) = 1.77 × 10−9 C/m²
E = D/ε0 = 1.77 × 10−9/8.854 × 10−12 = 200 V/m
∮D · dS = D(4πr²) = (1.77 × 10−9)(1.131) = 2.0 × 10−9 C = Q ✓

The D-flux is in coulombs and equals the enclosed charge. If you instead compute the E-flux, ΦE = ∮E · dS = Q/ε0 = 226 V·m. Keep the two fluxes separate: D is the one that counts free charge directly.

Magnetic check: enclose an entire bar magnet with a closed surface. If 0.80 mWb leaves near the north face, 0.80 mWb enters near the south face, and the net is zero. That is ∇ · B = 0 in one line.

Common mistake: ∇ · B = 0 does not mean B = 0. It means the net flux through any closed surface is zero. A region can have a strong, uniform B and still satisfy the law, because every field line that enters the region leaves it. Also, Qenc is only the charge inside the surface; a charge just outside contributes zero net flux, since its lines enter and leave the same closed surface.

5.4Charge continuity: the books must balance

Charge is not created or destroyed. If more current leaves a closed surface than enters, the charge inside must fall. That sentence is the continuity equation, and it is the reason Maxwell's displacement term has to exist.

Take a fixed volume V bounded by a closed surface S. The outward current is i(t) = ∮S J · dS. The charge inside is Q = ∫V ρv dV. If current flows out, the inside charge must decrease:

∮S J · dS = − dQ/dt = − d/dt ∫V ρv dV

The volume is stationary, so the derivative moves inside as a partial derivative. Apply the divergence theorem to the left side, and use the fact that the volume is arbitrary:

∫V ∇ · J dV = − ∫V ∂ρv/∂t dV   ⇒   ∇ · J + ∂ρv/∂t = 0
Why the displacement current is forced. Take the divergence of ∇ × H = Jc + ∂D/∂t. The divergence of a curl is zero, so 0 = ∇ · Jc + ∂(∇ · D)/∂t = ∇ · Jc + ∂ρv/∂t. That is exactly continuity. Without the ∂D/∂t term, Ampère's law would contradict charge conservation whenever the charge density changes. Maxwell did not add the term by taste; the books forced it.
Current in, current outbalance the two sliders and watch dQ/dt cross zero

Example 5.5 — recovering ρv from a current density

Given J = (x² x̂ − 4y² ŷ + 2xz ẑ) cos(ωt) A/m², find the volume charge density ρv that satisfies continuity.

Step 1 — divergence. Differentiate each component with respect to its own coordinate:

∂Jx/∂x = ∂(x² cos ωt)/∂x = 2x cos ωt
∂Jy/∂y = ∂(−4y² cos ωt)/∂y = −8y cos ωt
∂Jz/∂z = ∂(2xz cos ωt)/∂z = 2x cos ωt
∇ · J = (2x − 8y + 2x) cos ωt = (4x − 8y) cos ωt A/m³

Step 2 — continuity. Set ∂ρv/∂t = −∇ · J:

∂ρv/∂t = −(4x − 8y) cos ωt = (8y − 4x) cos ωt

Step 3 — integrate in time. Hold x, y, z fixed:

ρv = ∫(8y − 4x) cos(ωt) dt = ((8y − 4x)/ω) sin(ωt) + C

Check. Differentiate the answer: ∂ρv/∂t = (8y − 4x) cos ωt = −∇ · J. ✓

The constant. At t = 0, sin(ωt) = 0, so C = ρv(0): the charge density present before the time-varying field was switched on. If the medium is neutral at t = 0, C = 0. If it carries a static background charge, keep C; it does not affect the alternating part. A numerical snapshot: at x = 1 m, y = 2 m, ω = 1.0 × 106 rad/s, ωt = 1.0 rad, and C = 0, ρv = ((16 − 4)/106) sin(1) = 1.01 × 10−5 C/m³ ≈ 10 µC/m³.

Common mistakes: sign, constant, and units. (1) With the outward normal, ∮J · dS = −dQ/dt. If you write +dQ/dt, you have chosen an inward normal and every sign after that is wrong. (2) Do not drop C; it is the initial charge density, and a question that says “initially neutral” is telling you to set it to zero. (3) ∇ · J is in A/m³. Integrating cos(ωt) in time brings a factor 1/ω, whose unit is seconds, so ρv comes out in C/m³. If your answer is still in A/m³, you forgot to integrate with respect to time.
Exam link. Final 2024–25, Q1 asks directly for the differential form of the continuity equation: ∇ · J + ∂ρv/∂t = 0. That is a 0.5-mark recall question, but the same identity drives the longer “given J, find ρv” problem. Write the three steps — divergence, sign flip, time integral — and the arithmetic is routine.

5.5Constitutive relations and the full set

Maxwell's four equations contain five field quantities — E, D, B, H, and Jc — plus ρv. The constitutive relations are what adapt the equations to the material. They are not new physics; they are the material's response to the fields.

D = εE   ·   B = μH   ·   Jc = σE

ε is the permittivity (F/m), μ is the permeability (H/m), and σ is the conductivity (S/m). Free space is the reference case: ε0 = 8.854 × 10−12 F/m, μ0 = 4π × 10−7 H/m, σ = 0. The two constants also give the speed of light, c = 1/√(μ0ε0) ≈ 3 × 108 m/s, and the free-space impedance, η0 = √(μ0/ε0) = 120π ≈ 377 Ω. Chapter 6 uses both.

MediumεμσWhat it does to the fields
Free spaceε0μ00No charge, no current; waves travel at c
Perfect dielectricεrε0μrμ00Bound charge only; no steady conduction current
Conductorεμ> 0J = σE; free charge moves; waves attenuate
Good conductorεμσ/(ωε) ≫ 1Conduction dominates; skin depth δ = 1/√(πfμσ)

The same three parameters also classify a medium: homogeneous if ε, μ, σ do not depend on position; isotropic if D is parallel to E and B parallel to H; linear if the relations above hold without field-dependent coefficients; dispersive if ε or μ depends on frequency. A non-linear or anisotropic medium needs tensors, which is beyond this chapter's exam scope.

Two fast checks. In a dielectric with εr = 4 and E = 10 kV/m, D = ε0εrE = (8.854 × 10−12)(4)(104) = 3.54 × 10−7 C/m². In copper with σ = 5.8 × 107 S/m and E = 0.010 V/m, J = σE = 5.8 × 105 A/m². Both are one-line substitutions, and both expose a unit error immediately if ε0 or σ is missing.

Notation: ΦB = ∫B · dS, ΦD = ∫D · dS, Ienc = ∫Jc · dS. The integral form is for a chosen surface or loop; the differential form is the same law at a point. Divergence and Stokes theorems convert one into the other.

Continuity is not a fifth Maxwell equation. It follows from the four: take the divergence of Ampère–Maxwell and substitute Gauss's law. The full set is four equations plus three constitutive relations, and continuity is the consistency condition that ties them together. If a solution violates ∇ · J + ∂ρv/∂t = 0, it is not a physical solution.

Common mistake: units at the differential level. Every term in a point equation must have the same units. ∇ · D is C/m³, same as ρv. ∇ × H is A/m², same as Jc and ∂D/∂t. ∇ × E is V/m², same as ∂B/∂t (T/s). How to route a Maxwell question. A closed surface → Gauss. A closed loop → Faraday if the flux changes, Ampère–Maxwell if there is current or a changing D. Given J and asked for ρv → continuity. Given a material and asked for D, B, or J → constitutive. Given a wave and asked for E from H → Ampère–Maxwell in differential form, then integrate in time.

5.6Check yourself

Three questions. Answer first, then reveal. If you miss one, the solution names the section to reread.

Quiz 1 · Faraday and Lenz

A circular loop lies in the plane of the page. A uniform magnetic field points into the page and is decreasing. What is the direction of the induced current?

Show answer
The external flux into the page is decreasing. By Lenz's law the induced current must produce flux into the page to oppose the decrease. The right-hand rule then gives a clockwise current. If the field were increasing, the answer would be counter-clockwise. See §5.1.
Quiz 2 · displacement current

A 100 pF capacitor is charged so that dV/dt = 2.0 × 105 V/s. What is the displacement current?

Show answer
Id = C dV/dt = (100 × 10−12)(2.0 × 105) = 2.0 × 10−5 A = 20 µA. The displacement current is not zero just because the gap has no charge crossing it; it is set by the rate of change of the flux. See §5.2.
Quiz 3 · continuity

For J = (x² x̂ − 4y² ŷ + 2xz ẑ) cos(ωt), which ρv satisfies continuity? C is a constant.

Show answer
∇ · J = (2x − 8y + 2x) cos ωt = (4x − 8y) cos ωt. Continuity gives ∂ρv/∂t = −∇ · J = (8y − 4x) cos ωt. Integrating in time gives ρv = ((8y − 4x)/ω) sin ωt + C. The sign flip is the step most people miss. See §5.4.
