Chapter 01 · Vector analysis

Vectors & coordinate systems

Every electromagnetic quantity — force, field, flux — has a direction, and every integral in this course is written in one of three coordinate systems. This chapter builds the algebra, the three systems, the six transformation matrices, and the differential elements. The control opens with exactly this skill: rewrite a uniform field in cylindrical and spherical coordinates.

Notation. Vectors carry an arrow (A⃗); unit vectors carry a hat (x̂, ρ̂, r̂). The same symbol without a mark is its magnitude.

1.1 · Scalars, vectors, and why EMF is vector-heavy

An electric field at a point is not a number. It is an arrow: a magnitude in V/m and a direction. The force on a charge is another arrow, F⃗ = qE⃗ + qv⃗ × B⃗. Flux is a dot product because only the field crossing a surface matters. Circulation is a cross product because rotation is measured perpendicular to the loop. That is why this course begins with arrows rather than with Maxwell's equations.

The exam pays for this immediately. The 2024–25 control gives 1.25 points for rewriting F = 10x̂ in cylindrical and spherical components, before any field has been computed. Later flux and charge integrals depend just as directly on the differential elements built in this chapter.

Vector and unit vector. A vector is written A⃗ = A âA, where A = |A⃗| is the magnitude and âA is a unit vector in the direction of A⃗. A unit vector carries direction only:
âA = A⃗ / |A⃗|,   |âA| = 1,   A⃗ = Ax x̂ + Ay ŷ + Az ẑ
|A⃗| = √(Ax² + Ay² + Az²)

Addition and subtraction. Add components, never magnitudes. Place the tail of B⃗ at the tip of A⃗; the arrow from the origin to the new tip is A⃗ + B⃗. The same diagonal is obtained by starting with B⃗, so addition is commutative. Subtraction is addition of the negative: A⃗ − B⃗ points from the tip of B⃗ to the tip of A⃗.

Vector additionmove the four component sliders

Position vector. The position vector of a point P is the arrow from the origin O to P, written R⃗ = OP. In Cartesian coordinates R⃗ = x x̂ + y ŷ + z ẑ, and its magnitude is the distance OP. In cylindrical and spherical coordinates this arrow collapses to two or one terms — a simplification used constantly in Chapter 3. The position vector is not a general vector: it always starts at the origin.

Worked example 1
Magnitude and unit vector of A = 3x̂ + 4ŷ + 12ẑ
|A⃗| = √(3² + 4² + 12²) = √(9 + 16 + 144) = √169 = 13
âA = (3/13) x̂ + (4/13) ŷ + (12/13) ẑ ≈ 0.2308 x̂ + 0.3077 ŷ + 0.9231 ẑ

Check: 0.2308² + 0.3077² + 0.9231² = 0.0533 + 0.0947 + 0.8521 = 1.0001 ≈ 1. The vector has length 13; the unit vector has length 1.

Common mistakes.
  • Adding magnitudes. |A⃗ + B⃗| = |A⃗| + |B⃗| only when the vectors are parallel and point the same way. Otherwise the triangle inequality gives a smaller result.
  • Dividing by one component. Normalise with the full magnitude: âA = A⃗/|A⃗|, not A⃗/Ax.
  • Reversing a subtraction. A⃗ − B⃗ = −(B⃗ − A⃗). The direction of the arrow is part of the answer.
  • Forgetting the units. A unit vector is dimensionless; a field vector is not.
Source check. In the lecture's Example 1.4, A⃗ = B⃗ − C⃗ with B⃗ = h ẑ and C⃗ = R0 ρ̂ gives A⃗ = h ẑ − R0 ρ̂. The printed final line uses R0 ρ̂ − h ẑ, which is −A⃗. The unit vector is (h ẑ − R0 ρ̂)/√(h² + R0²); the sign fixes the direction.

1.2 · Dot, cross, and the two triple products

Two vectors have two useful products. The dot product answers “how much of B⃗ lies along A⃗?” and returns a scalar. The cross product answers “how much area do A⃗ and B⃗ span, and which way does that area face?” and returns a vector. The triple products combine them into a volume and into a vector that lies in the plane of two of the three inputs.

Dot product — projection, angle, work

A⃗ · B⃗ = AB cos θ = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz

The result is a number, so the dot product is also called the scalar product. It is commutative and distributive. Geometrically it multiplies the length of one vector by the component of the other along it. That component is the scalar projection:

B cos θ = (A⃗ · B⃗) / A = B⃗ · âA
vector projection of B⃗ on A⃗ = (B cos θ) âA = (B⃗ · âA) âA

Rearranging the definition gives the angle between two vectors. This is the fastest way to test whether two fields are perpendicular: if the dot product is zero, the angle is 90°.

cos θ = (A⃗ · B⃗) / (AB),   A⃗ · B⃗ = 0 ⇔ θ = 90°

Work and flux. Mechanical work is W = F⃗ · d⃗: only the force along the displacement does work. The same structure appears in electrostatics, where the flux through a surface is Φ = ∫ D⃗ · dS⃗. The dot product selects the component normal to the surface. A field parallel to a surface contributes zero flux, however strong it is.

Dot product and projectiondrag θ past 90° and watch the sign flip
Worked example 2
Angle and projection for A = 3x̂ + 4ŷ + 12ẑ and B = 2x̂ + 3ŷ + 6ẑ
A⃗ · B⃗ = (3)(2) + (4)(3) + (12)(6) = 6 + 12 + 72 = 90
|A⃗| = 13,   |B⃗| = √(4 + 9 + 36) = 7
cos θ = 90 / (13 × 7) = 90/91 = 0.98901 → θ = 8.50°
B cos θ = 90/13 = 6.92

The two vectors point almost the same way: the angle is only 8.5°, so the dot product is close to AB = 91. The projection of B on A is 6.92, slightly less than |B| = 7.

Cross product — area, normal, right-hand rule

A⃗ × B⃗ = (AB sin θ) ân
ân = (âA × âB) / |sin θ|

The direction of A⃗ × B⃗ is normal to the plane containing the two vectors and follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers from A⃗ toward B⃗; the thumb points along the product. The magnitude AB sin θ is the area of the parallelogram with sides A⃗ and B⃗. Half of that is the area of the triangle they form.

In Cartesian components the cross product is a determinant. Expand along the first row, remembering the minus sign on the middle term:

A⃗ × B⃗ = x̂ŷẑ AxAyAz BxByBz = (AyBz − AzBy) x̂ − (AxBz − AzBx) ŷ + (AxBy − AyBx) ẑ

The cross product is anticommutative: A⃗ × B⃗ = −B⃗ × A⃗. It is distributive over addition, but it is not associative. Two nonzero vectors give zero exactly when they are parallel (θ = 0 or 180°), because sin θ = 0. This is the test for collinearity.

Where it appears. Torque is τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗. The magnetic force on a moving charge is F⃗ = q v⃗ × B⃗, which is why a charge moving parallel to a magnetic field feels no force. Chapter 4 uses that cross product on every page; the 2024–25 control already asks for its magnitude in Question 3.

Scalar and vector triple products

The scalar triple product A⃗ · (B⃗ × C⃗) is a number. Its absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped with edges A⃗, B⃗ and C⃗. The parentheses matter: compute the cross product first, then dot the result with the remaining vector.

A⃗ · (B⃗ × C⃗) = AxAyAz BxByBz CxCyCz
A⃗ · (B⃗ × C⃗) = B⃗ · (C⃗ × A⃗) = C⃗ · (A⃗ × B⃗)

The cyclic swap leaves the value unchanged; swapping any two vectors flips the sign. If the triple product is zero, the three vectors are coplanar and the “volume” collapses to a plane.

The vector triple product A⃗ × (B⃗ × C⃗) is a vector. It lies in the plane of B⃗ and C⃗, not in the plane of A⃗ and B⃗. Expand it with the “BAC − CAB” rule:

A⃗ × (B⃗ × C⃗) = B⃗(A⃗ · C⃗) − C⃗(A⃗ · B⃗)
(A⃗ × B⃗) × C⃗ = B⃗(A⃗ · C⃗) − A⃗(B⃗ · C⃗)

The two expressions are different in general, so the cross product is not associative. Always keep the parentheses.

Worked example 4
Triple products with A = x̂ + 2ŷ + 2ẑ, B = 2x̂ + ŷ, C = 3ŷ + ẑ
B⃗ × C⃗ = (1·1 − 0·3) x̂ − (2·1 − 0·0) ŷ + (2·3 − 1·0) ẑ = x̂ − 2ŷ + 6ẑ
A⃗ · (B⃗ × C⃗) = 1(1) + 2(−2) + 2(6) = 1 − 4 + 12 = 9 → volume = 9

A⃗ · C⃗ = 0 + 6 + 2 = 8,   A⃗ · B⃗ = 2 + 2 + 0 = 4
A⃗ × (B⃗ × C⃗) = 8B⃗ − 4C⃗ = (16, 8, 0) − (0, 12, 4) = 16x̂ − 4ŷ − 4ẑ

Direct check: A⃗ × (x̂ − 2ŷ + 6ẑ) = x̂(2·6 − 2(−2)) − ŷ(1·6 − 2·1) + ẑ(1(−2) − 2·1) = 16x̂ − 4ŷ − 4ẑ. The scalar triple is a number; the vector triple is a vector.

Common mistakes.
  • Dropping the minus. A⃗ × B⃗ = −B⃗ × A⃗. A sign error here changes a force direction and usually costs the whole question.
  • Reading the determinant left to right. The middle term is subtracted: −(AxBz − AzBx) ŷ.
  • Adding a unit vector to a scalar. A⃗ · B⃗ is a number; it cannot be “x̂ + 3”.
  • Mixing degrees and radians. Check the calculator mode before evaluating sin θ or cos θ.
  • Writing (A⃗ · B⃗) × C⃗. That is a scalar crossed with a vector, which is not defined. If you mean scaling, write (A⃗ · B⃗)C⃗.

1.3 · Cartesian, cylindrical, spherical

A coordinate system is a labelling scheme for points plus a set of local directions. An orthogonal system has axes that meet at right angles at every point. All three systems below are orthogonal and right-handed. The physics does not change when you switch systems; the difficulty of the integral does. Choosing the system that matches the symmetry is the single highest-leverage move in this course.

Cartesian (rectangular): (x, y, z)

Three distances along three fixed perpendicular axes. The unit vectors x̂, ŷ, ẑ have the same direction at every point, which is why Cartesian components are the easiest to add and differentiate.

Ranges: −∞ < x, y, z < +∞.   Cyclic: x̂ × ŷ = ẑ, ŷ × ẑ = x̂, ẑ × x̂ = ŷ.   Position: R⃗ = x x̂ + y ŷ + z ẑ.

Use it when the boundary is a plane x = const, y = const or z = const: boxes, infinite sheets, straight wires along an axis, or fields with no angular symmetry.

Cylindrical: (ρ, φ, z)

ρ is the distance from the z-axis, not from the origin. φ is the azimuth measured from the positive x-axis in the xy-plane, counter-clockwise. z is the same height as in Cartesian coordinates.

Ranges: 0 ≤ ρ < ∞,  0 ≤ φ < 2π,  −∞ < z < +∞.   Unit vectors: ρ̂ points away from the z-axis; φ̂ points in the direction of increasing φ; ẑ is vertical. ρ̂ and φ̂ depend on φ.
Cyclic: ρ̂ × φ̂ = ẑ, φ̂ × ẑ = ρ̂, ẑ × ρ̂ = φ̂.   Position: R⃗ = ρ ρ̂ + z ẑ. There is no φ̂ term.

Use it when the boundary is a cylinder ρ = const: infinite line charges, coaxial cables, round wires along z, or anything with rotational symmetry about the z-axis.

Spherical: (r, θ, φ)

r is the distance from the origin. θ is the polar (zenith) angle measured from the positive z-axis; it is not the angle in the xy-plane. φ is the same azimuth as in cylindrical coordinates.

Ranges: 0 ≤ r < ∞,  0 ≤ θ ≤ π,  0 ≤ φ < 2π.   Unit vectors: r̂ points radially outward; θ̂ points toward increasing θ (south along the meridian); φ̂ points east. All three depend on θ and φ.
Cyclic: r̂ × θ̂ = φ̂, θ̂ × φ̂ = r̂, φ̂ × r̂ = θ̂.   Position: R⃗ = r r̂.

Use it when the boundary is a sphere r = const or a cone θ = const: point charges, spherical shells, cones, or any problem with a point of symmetry at the origin.

Choosing in five seconds. Look at the boundary, not at the field. A surface described by x = const is Cartesian. A surface described by ρ = const is cylindrical. A surface described by r = const or θ = const is spherical. If two systems fit, choose the one that makes the limits constants.
Common mistakes.
  • φ measured from the z-axis. The lecture's §I.3.2 sentence says this; it is a slip. φ is measured from the positive x-axis in the xy-plane. θ is the angle measured from +z.
  • Confusing ρ and r. ρ = √(x² + y²) is the distance from the z-axis. r = √(x² + y² + z²) is the distance from the origin. They are equal only in the plane z = 0.
  • Adding a φ̂ term to the position vector. In cylindrical coordinates R⃗ = ρ ρ̂ + z ẑ. Moving in φ does not move the point away from the origin, so there is no φ̂ component.
  • Adding vectors given at different points. ρ̂, φ̂, r̂ and θ̂ are local. A vector at P and a vector at Q must be transformed to a common point before they are added.
  • Wrong θ range. θ runs from 0 to π, not 0 to 2π. φ is the coordinate that goes all the way around.
Source check. The spherical cyclic list in the lecture ends with φ̂ × r̂ = φ̂; the correct third relation is φ̂ × r̂ = θ̂. The cyclic order is r̂ → θ̂ → φ̂ → r̂.

1.4 · Coordinate and unit-vector transformations

A point has one location but three possible descriptions. Convert the coordinates first, then convert the components. Mixing the two steps is the usual source of wrong answers: the matrix entries depend on the angles at the point, so the angles must be known before the matrix is applied.

Coordinate conversions

ρ = √(x² + y²),   φ = tan−1(y/x),   z = z
r = √(x² + y² + z²) = √(ρ² + z²),   θ = cos−1(z/r),   φ = tan−1(y/x)
x = ρ cos φ = r sin θ cos φ,   y = ρ sin φ = r sin θ sin φ,   z = r cos θ
The inverse tangent is not enough. tan−1(y/x) cannot tell (1, 1) from (−1, −1). Use the quadrant: on paper, sketch the point; in code, use atan2(y, x). The range 0 ≤ φ < 2π fixes the convention used here.

The control Q1: F = 10x̂ in cylindrical and spherical coordinates

Exam link. Control 2024–25 Q1 · 1.25 pts — a uniform field of magnitude 10 points along +x. Part (a) asks for its cylindrical components; part (b) asks for its spherical components. The marker wants the matrix, the multiplication and the final vector, not a guessed answer.

Start in Cartesian: F⃗ = 10 x̂ + 0 ŷ + 0 ẑ, so the component column is (10, 0, 0). The field is uniform, but its cylindrical and spherical components are not constant: the basis vectors rotate from point to point.

Worked example 5 · Q1(a)
Cartesian to cylindrical
FρFφFz = cos φsin φ0 −sin φcos φ0 001 1000 = 10 cos φ−10 sin φ0
F⃗ = 10 cos φ ρ̂ − 10 sin φ φ̂ = 10(cos φ ρ̂ − sin φ φ̂)

Check the magnitude: |F| = 10√(cos²φ + sin²φ) = 10. At φ = 0 the point is on the +x axis, where ρ̂ = x̂, so F = 10ρ̂. At φ = 90° it is on the +y axis, where x̂ = −φ̂, so F = −10φ̂.

Worked example 6 · Q1(b)
Cartesian to spherical
FrFθFφ = sin θ cos φsin θ sin φcos θ cos θ cos φcos θ sin φ−sin θ −sin φcos φ0 1000 = 10 sin θ cos φ10 cos θ cos φ−10 sin φ
F⃗ = 10[sin θ cos φ r̂ + cos θ cos φ θ̂ − sin φ φ̂]

Magnitude check: 100(sin²θ cos²φ + cos²θ cos²φ + sin²φ) = 100(cos²φ + sin²φ) = 100. At θ = 90°, φ = 0 the point is on the +x axis, where r̂ = x̂ and θ̂ = −ẑ, so F = 10r̂, as required.

Cross-check through cylindrical. The same answer must come out of the C→S matrix. With (Fρ, Fφ, Fz) = (10 cos φ, −10 sin φ, 0):

Fr = sin θ (10 cos φ) + 0 + cos θ (0) = 10 sin θ cos φ
Fθ = cos θ (10 cos φ) + 0 − sin θ (0) = 10 cos θ cos φ
Fφ = 0 + 1(−10 sin φ) + 0 = −10 sin φ

The two routes agree. Use this as a self-check in the exam: if your cylindrical and spherical answers do not describe the same physical vector, one matrix was applied with the wrong angles.

Common mistakes.
  • Using the wrong direction. Converting Cartesian → cylindrical uses R→C, whose first row is (cos φ, sin φ, 0). Multiplying by C→R instead gives a different vector unless φ = 0.
  • Forgetting that the matrix depends on the point. The entries contain φ and θ. Plug in the angles of the point where the vector is evaluated, not the angles of some other point.
  • Converting coordinates but keeping components. A point and a vector are different objects. Convert (x, y, z) to (ρ, φ, z), then apply the matrix to (Ax, Ay, Az).
  • Expecting constant components. A uniform Cartesian field has φ- and θ-dependent curvilinear components. That is correct, not a mistake.
  • Losing a sign in φ. sin φ is positive in the first and second quadrants, negative in the third and fourth. Use atan2 and keep the quadrant.

1.5 · Differential length, volume and surface

An integral is a sum over tiny pieces. In a curvilinear system the pieces are not all the same size: a step dφ moves you farther when ρ or r is large. The metric factors ρ, r and r sin θ convert coordinate steps into physical lengths. Miss one and the integral is dimensionally wrong even if every limit is right.

Where the factors come from. In cylindrical coordinates a small change dφ traces an arc of length ρ dφ, so the φ-direction length element is ρ dφ. The volume is the product of the three edge lengths: dρ, ρ dφ and dz. In spherical coordinates the θ-arc is r dθ and the φ-arc is r sin θ dφ; the volume is dr · r dθ · r sin θ dφ = r² sin θ dr dθ dφ. Each surface element uses the two edge lengths on its face, with the unit vector normal to that face.

Check the units. dℓ must have units of length. dv must be length³. Every dS⃗ must be length². If a candidate element has the wrong power of length, a metric factor is missing.

Worked example 9
Charge in a sphere with ρv = 4 cos²θ C/cm³

A sphere of radius 2 cm carries a volume charge density ρv = 4 cos²θ C/cm³. The density depends on θ, so it cannot be pulled out of the θ-integral.

Units: C/cm³ × cm³ = C. Check the density: it is maximum on the z-axis (θ = 0, π) and zero on the equator (θ = π/2). The sin θ factor in dv still gives a finite total because the equatorial band has the most volume.

The surface integral uses dS⃗ = r² sin θ dθ dφ r̂ on the outer sphere; the limits on θ and φ come straight from the region. Getting dS⃗ and the limits right is most of the six points.
Common mistakes.
  • Dropping ρ or r. dℓφ = ρ dφ, not dφ; dvspherical = r² sin θ dr dθ dφ, not dr dθ dφ.
  • Using the wrong surface factor. dSθ = r sin θ dr dφ (one power of r), while dSr = r² sin θ dθ dφ (two powers).
  • Integrating θ to 2π. θ runs 0 to π; φ runs 0 to 2π.
  • Mixing centimetres and metres. Convert every length to the same unit before multiplying; the charge example above stays in cm and reports C because ρv is per cm³.
  • Forgetting that dS⃗ is a vector. For a closed surface use the outward normal; the sign of the flux depends on it.

Check 1 · position vector

Which expression is the position vector of a point P(ρ, φ, z) in cylindrical coordinates?

Show answer
The arrow from the origin to P has a horizontal part ρ ρ̂ and a vertical part z ẑ. Increasing φ moves P around the z-axis but does not change its distance from the origin, so there is no φ̂ term. Answer: a.
Check 2 · angle from a dot product

A⃗ = 3x̂ + 4ŷ and B⃗ = −4x̂ + 3ŷ. What is the angle between them?

Show answer
A⃗ · B⃗ = (3)(−4) + (4)(3) = −12 + 12 = 0. A zero dot product means cos θ = 0, so θ = 90°. The two vectors are perpendicular. Answer: c.
Check 3 · the control conversion

F⃗ = 10x̂. Which expression gives F⃗ in cylindrical coordinates?

Show answer
Use R→C with (Ax, Ay, Az) = (10, 0, 0). The first row gives Fρ = 10 cos φ; the second gives Fφ = −10 sin φ; Fz = 0. Hence F⃗ = 10(cos φ ρ̂ − sin φ φ̂). At φ = 0 this is 10ρ̂, as it must be on the +x axis. Answer: b.
