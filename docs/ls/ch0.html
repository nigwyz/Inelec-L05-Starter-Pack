The structure to memorise. Two thirds of the control is Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 material (properties + convolution), one third is Chapter 3 (Fourier series). Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 appear in the make-up and in the recitations, but the 2023 and 2024–25 controls stop at the Fourier series. If you are short on time, Chapter 1 + Chapter 3 first, then Chapter 2, then the Z-transform.
Negative marking on True/False. The 2023 paper says explicitly: “note: a wrong answer will be counted negatively”. Guessing on that block is worse than leaving it. Only mark what you can justify — and be ready to justify it in one line.
How the marks are actually spent (from the recovered solutions)

Look at a marked solution and the pattern is consistent: each of the four properties in Exercise 2 is worth roughly a point, split between stating the test and applying it. A bare “the system is not linear” earns nothing; “linearity: T{ax₁+bx₂} = (n+1)(ax₁[n−1]+bx₂[n−1]) = a·y₁ + b·y₂ ⇒ linear” earns the point. Write the test, then the substitution, then the verdict.

The same rule applies in Exercise 4: the convolution is worth the plot, not just the algebra — always plot the result, label the axes and the index of the peak.

4Eight rules that decide most marks

  1. Every property needs a test, not a verdict. “Not linear” is worth nothing; T{ax₁+bx₂} = a·T{x₁} + b·T{x₂} — violated by one concrete pair — is worth the point.
  2. Causal means h[n] = 0 for n < 0. Stable means Σ|h[n]| < ∞. They are independent: a causal system can be unstable (h[n]=u[n]) and a stable system can be non-causal (h[n]=(1/2)ⁿu[−n−1]).
  3. A finite-duration impulse response is always stable — this is why the 2023 True/False block has it.
  4. Convolution support: if x[n] lives on [n₁, n₂] and h[n] on [m₁, m₂], then y[n] lives on [n₁+m₁, n₂+m₂]. Draw it before computing it.
  5. In a convolution, the number of overlapping terms changes with n. That is exactly why (1/3)ⁿu[n] * (1/3)ⁿu[n] gives (n+1)(1/3)ⁿ and not a geometric sum. The overlap length is n+1.
  6. Parseval for DFS: Σₙ₌₀^{N−1}|x[n]|² = N·Σₖ₌₀^{N−1}|aₖ|². The factor N is the single most-dropped symbol in this course.
  7. Real signal ⇒ conjugate symmetry: a₋ₖ = aₖ*. So for a real signal only a₀ (and a_{N/2} when N is even) are forced to be real — a real periodic signal can still have complex coefficients. This is 2023 True/False 4.
  8. Always draw the answer. Convolution (2023 Ex 4), DFS synthesis (2024–25 Ex 3) and every impulse response are scored on the plot as well as the algebra.

5Ten-minute diagnostic

Five questions, one from each high-value skill. Try them closed-book, then open the solution. Every question here is a real control question or a close relative of one.

2023 control · Ex 1.2
The impulse response of a causal LTI system satisfies Σₙ |h[n]|² < ∞. True or false?
Solution
False. Causality is a statement about where the impulse response lives (h[n] = 0 for n < 0), not about its size. h[n] = u[n] is causal and has Σ|h[n]| = ∞, so it is unstable. The energy condition Σ|h[n]|² < ∞ is a stability criterion, and a causal LTI system is stable only if it happens to satisfy it as well.
2023 control · Ex 2.1
Is y[n] = (n+1)x[n−1] time-invariant?
Solution
No. Shift the input by n₀: T{x[n−n₀]} = (n+1)x[n−n₀−1]. Shift the output instead: y[n−n₀] = (n−n₀+1)x[n−n₀−1]. These differ by n₀, so the system is not time-invariant (it is linear, and causal, and unstable). The rule of thumb: a factor that depends explicitly on n breaks time-invariance.
2023 control · Ex 3.2
A periodic sequence of period N = 5 has DFS coefficients aₖ = jᵏ (−1 ≤ k ≤ 1) and aₖ = −1 (k = ±2). What is Σₙ₌₀⁴ |x[n]|²?
Solution
25. Parseval: Σₙ₌₀⁴|x[n]|² = N·Σₖ₌₀⁴|aₖ|². The five coefficients are 1, j, −j, −1, −1, so Σ|aₖ|² = 1+1+1+1+1 = 5, and 5 × 5 = 25. Notice you never need x[n] itself — that is the whole point of Parseval.
2024–25 control · Ex 4
x[n] = h[n] = (1/3)ⁿu[n]. What is y[n] = x[n]*h[n]?
Solution
(n+1)(1/3)ⁿu[n]. y[n] = Σₖ x[k]h[n−k]. Both sequences are right-sided, so k runs from 0 to n — n+1 terms, each equal to (1/3)ⁿ. Hence y[n] = (n+1)(1/3)ⁿ for n ≥ 0 and 0 otherwise. Option (a) is the trap in the archived handwritten solution: it replaces a sum whose length grows with n by an infinite geometric series, which is invalid.
2023 control · Ex 5
For a periodic signal with aₖ = −a_{N−ₖ} and N = 8, what does y[n] = ((4+(−1)ⁿ)/2)·x[n−1] do to the coefficients?
Solution
Even bins doubled, odd bins vanish (plus the one-bin shift from x[n−1]). (4+(−1)ⁿ)/2 = 2 + (1/2)(−1)ⁿ. The term (1/2)(−1)ⁿ has DFS coefficients (1/2)(−1)ᵏ, so it kills the odd k and doubles the even k. The x[n−1] shift multiplies every coefficient by e^{−j2πk/N}. Chapter 3 works the algebra out.

6How to spend the last days

If you have one full day

  • Morning (3 h) — Chapter 1 in full, with the visual panels. Do not skip Exercise 2 of the 2023 paper.
  • Midday (2 h) — Chapter 3 (DFS + Parseval + coefficient manipulation).
  • Afternoon (2 h) — Chapter 2 (difference equations, cascades, stability).
  • Evening (2 h) — Chapter 5 (Z-transform, ROC) and the formula sheet from memory.
  • Last hour — the practice page, timed: one full control in 60 minutes, then check every mark you lost.

If you have only three hours

  • Hour 1 — Chapter 1 property rules and the convolution support rule, plus the diagnostic above.
  • Hour 2 — Chapter 3: synthesis, Parseval, and the three coefficient-manipulation identities.
  • Hour 3 — redo the five diagnostic questions closed-book, then read the formula sheet once.

In the three-hour plan, Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 are the ones you sacrifice — they are worth the least in the controls and cost the most to learn from scratch.

Recitations 1–3 are the drills, not the syllabus. Recitation 1 is signals and properties, Recitation 2 is convolution and cascade systems, Recitation 3 is how to decide linearity and time-invariance from observed input/output pairs. Together they cover most of the control. Recitation 4 (Fourier series) and Recitation 5 (DFT and Z-transform) map onto Chapters 3 and 5.
